## Question, method and result

The randomised study covers more than 600 Indonesian village road projects. Raising government audit probability from 4% to 100% reduced missing expenditure by approximately eight percentage points. Discrepancies between reports and independent engineering estimates were measured separately from the audit itself. Increased citizen monitoring had a small average effect. This is a peer-reviewed field evaluation of specific interventions, not a comparison of all centralised and local governance arrangements.

## ORI critical analysis

Separating intervention from outcome measurement is a strength. Measuring audit success only by detected violations could make better oversight appear to worsen corruption. Independent estimation of material expenditure supplies a different indicator. Nevertheless, discrepancies cannot unconditionally be treated as exact corruption amounts: normal losses, prices and measurement errors matter. Group comparisons require those errors not to change systematically with treatment.

A small average effect of citizen monitoring does not establish participation’s uselessness. Participation can serve representation or legitimacy, neither measured by road expenditure. Oversight also depends on information access, collective-action capacity and consequences after a problem is found. Holding a meeting cannot substitute for these conditions.

## Programme relevance

For ORI, the paper models a concrete accountability question: who checks, what is measured, what consequences follow, and which outcome is independent of providers’ reporting? Transfer to digital services would require another direct measure, such as completed procedures and corrected refusals. Scaling requires attention to monitoring costs, persistence and displacement into less observable misconduct. The experiment supports a local inference under specified conditions rather than a universal architecture for the state.
