## Content and form of argument

The publisher presents three categories of failure: basic, complex and intelligent. The central idea is that learning requires distinguishing preventable breakdowns from risks capable of producing new knowledge. The book combines a research perspective with stories and practical recommendations. Its public description cannot establish the comparative effectiveness of each recommendation or reconstruct the underlying studies. A scientific topic does not by itself make a book a causal evaluation of a management programme.

## ORI critical analysis

For organisational research, it helps to specify in advance what would make an unsuccessful experiment informative. An explicit assumption, bounded scale, observable outcome and subsequent decision rule are needed. If a team claims to have gained experience after every possible outcome, learning cannot be distinguished from rationalisation. Experiment quality should be assessed against information available beforehand, not only its eventual success.

A separate problem concerns reporting and the underlying incidence of mistakes. Following a cultural change, a team might report more failures even while performance improves. An increase in recorded incidents therefore cannot be read directly as deterioration. Additional measures should capture severity, recurring causes and speed of correction. Calm discussion of failure does not remove the obligation to eliminate a known hazard.

## Programme relevance

An AI pilot could distinguish execution failures in established processes, interaction failures and bounded trials of unfamiliar approaches. This is ORI’s proposed observation scheme, not a book-validated classification of every case. The next step is to locate primary studies of the chosen feedback mechanism and examine whether reviews change the process, who can report problems, and whether employees or customers bear the costs of experimentation.
