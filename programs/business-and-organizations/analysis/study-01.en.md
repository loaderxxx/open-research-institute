## Question, method and result

The working paper examines individual access to an assistant embedded in everyday workplace applications. The later abstract describes random assignment among 7,137 workers in 66 firms over six months. Users spent less time on email and outside regular working hours, while the authors detected no shift in task quantity or composition. The earlier PDF reports different time-saving estimates, making source version part of the result rather than a minor technical detail.

## ORI critical analysis

Randomly assigning access and examining active users are different analytical operations. Usage occurs after assignment and depends on employees’ needs. The average effect of offering the tool therefore cannot be replaced with a comparison of people who chose to use it and everyone else. A review should extract assignment and usage estimates separately, recording additional assumptions behind the latter.

Time savings also differ from increased output. A released hour may become rest, quality checking or activity missing from the recorded applications. Failure to detect a task shift does not demonstrate the absence of every organisational effect: individual randomisation may poorly test changes requiring collective agreement. Equally, it does not justify attributing such a transformation to the firm without observations.

## Programme relevance

The source connects AI adoption to coordination structures. Subsequent comparisons should distinguish autonomous tasks from tasks dependent on colleagues and measure output quality and after-hours workload. Licensing, training and verification costs require separate accounting. The work remains a preprint. Some authors’ Microsoft affiliations matter for assessing data independence and procedural disclosure, without substituting for examination of the design itself.
