## Books for orientation

- Oliver E. Williamson, [*The Economic Institutions of Capitalism: Firms, Markets, Relational Contracting*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Economic-Intstitutions-of-Capitalism/Oliver-E-Williamson/9780684863740), 1985 (Free Press reprint 1998), Free Press. A classic framework for transaction costs, contracts and firm boundaries; useful for asking when activity is organised inside a firm or through markets. The official Free Press record confirms the 1998 reprint and ISBN; book is paid or library access.
- James G. March & Herbert A. Simon, [*Organizations*](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Organizations%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9780631227400), 1958 (2nd ed. 1993), Wiley-Blackwell. Foundational work on bounded rationality, participation and organisational decisions; it connects individual behaviour with firm design. Publisher record is public; full text is generally paid or library access.
- Amy C. Edmondson, [*Right Kind of Wrong: The Science of Failing Well*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Right-Kind-of-Wrong/Amy-C-Edmondson/9781982195069), 2023, Atria Books. A practice-oriented account of distinguishing preventable from productive failure and learning in teams; relevant to feedback and experimentation, but not itself a causal evaluation. Publisher record is public; book is paid.

## Research and public data

- Dillon, Jaffe, Immorlica & Stanton, [“Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI”](https://doi.org/10.3386/w33795), 2025, NBER WP 33795. A randomised experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 workers found that Copilot users spent less time on email and after-hours work; individual access did not noticeably change task composition. Limits: large firms, an early tool version, Microsoft-affiliated authors and one product. Working-paper version and abstract are public; this is not a peer-reviewed journal article.
- Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, [“Generative AI at Work”](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044), 2025, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 140(2). Deployment data from 5,172 support agents show an average increase of about 15% in issues resolved, with larger gains for novices and weak or negative quality effects for the most experienced. Limit: one company and one customer-support setting. Abstract is public; full text may require a subscription.
- Hoffmann et al., [“Generative AI and the Nature of Work”](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/25-021_491efe26-e444-4e02-b58e-f27300cde12f.pdf), 2025, HBS Working Paper 25-021. Developer activity data and a GitHub Copilot eligibility threshold are used to estimate changes in task allocation; the focus is how AI changes the mix of work, not only speed. Limits: quasi-experimental design and a specific developer setting. Full preprint is public.
- Babina, [“Understanding Firms’ AI Efforts and Their Economic Impact”](https://academic.oup.com/rcfs/article/15/4/967/8742315), 2026, *Review of Corporate Finance Studies*. The title and publisher record were verified on Oxford Academic. The review stresses that patents, AI use, internal capability and investor expectations measure different things, so measure choice changes conclusions. It is a review, not a single causal estimate; the abstract and substantial text are available on the record, while full-text access may be restricted.
- Kaul, Anderson & McKenzie, [“Business Development Services and New Market Expansion: Evidence from a Field Experiment with Nigerian Entrepreneurs”](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5610873), 2025 preprint, revised 25 June 2026. A three-year randomized experiment with 1,011 established small businesses tested 88 hours of market-expansion support; the authors report increased entry into new customer segments and geographic regions, and firm growth linked to market expansion. Limits: one country, program and population; the results remain an SSRN preprint and have not undergone journal peer review. Abstract and manuscript are public; SSRN states reuse restrictions.
- Asghar & Obloj, [“Delegation of Decision Rights and Capital Reallocation: Evidence from US Municipalities”](https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.3708), 2025, *Strategic Management Journal* 46(7), 1700–1727. Using a change in decision rights in Illinois municipalities, the article estimates a causal effect of centralisation: more decentralised structures reallocated funds across functions more often, possibly by lowering information-processing burdens. Limit: US municipal setting; the publisher abstract is open, while full text may require a subscription.
- Legros & Newman, [“The ‘Value Principle’ in Management Practices, Organizational Design and Industrial Organization”](https://cepr.org/publications/dp20627), 2025, CEPR DP20627. A theoretical survey links product-market value to professional management, firm size and power, and internal control. Limit: theory rather than a single empirical dataset. Record and abstract are public; full text may be paid.

## Three historical landmarks

- **1776 — division of labour and production coordination.** Adam Smith, [*The Wealth of Nations*, Book I](https://www.econlib.org/library/Smith/smWN.html), describes specialisation and coordination through exchange. This is a starting point in economic thought, not modern firm-level evidence.
- **1937 — the firm as an alternative coordination mechanism.** Ronald Coase, [“The Nature of the Firm”](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x), explained firms through the costs of using the price mechanism and the entrepreneur's organisation of transactions. It is a theoretical article; later work disputes whether this explanation is complete.
- **1970s–1980s — transaction costs and agency relations.** Williamson developed a contractual account of firm boundaries; an official OUP record of the later research programme is available [here](https://academic.oup.com/book/51875). This is a family of explanations, not a universal law of company size.

## Researchers and teams active in the field

- **Erik Brynjolfsson — Stanford Graduate School of Business / Digital Economy Lab.** Studies productivity and digital technologies; a concrete recent contribution is [“Generative AI at Work”](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/generative-ai-work), published in *QJE* in 2025. [Institutional profile](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/faculty/erik-brynjolfsson).
- **Danielle Li — MIT Sloan, NBER.** Studies innovation economics, labour markets and AI; a concrete recent contribution is [“Generative AI at Work”](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044) with Erik Brynjolfsson and Lindsey Raymond (2025). Danielle Li is not an author of NBER w33795. [MIT Sloan profile](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/faculty/directory/danielle-li).
- **Christopher T. Stanton — Harvard Business School.** Studies organisational economics and technology at work; a concrete recent contribution is co-authorship of [“Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI”](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795) (2025). [HBS profile](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/profile.aspx?facId=602452).

## Current questions and next reading

In 2025–2026, research is moving from showing that AI can speed up a task to measuring changes in task mix, quality, learning, coordination and firm outcomes. Field estimates vary by experience and occupation; they do not support generalising a local effect to all firms. Open questions include whether effects persist after workflow adaptation, what happens to novice learning and expert skills, how to separate tool effects from firm and user selection, and how to measure quality and verification costs.

**Next reading:** read the abstracts and available versions of Coase (1937), Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) and Dillon et al. (2025), then compare units of analysis, counterfactuals and quality measures. A full review needs a documented search across entrepreneurship, incentives and organisational AI adoption.
