## 1. Subject of inquiry

Consciousness is often described as the presence of experience: there is something it is like for the experiencer to perceive a color, feel pain, or have a thought. This is a working pointer to the subject, not a laboratory definition that settles every dispute. Different authors also use “consciousness” to mean wakefulness, information available for speech, or the ability to monitor oneself. Mixing these meanings creates an appearance of agreement or contradiction where different phenomena are being studied.

A third party cannot directly measure subjective experience. Researchers compare reports, behavior, physiology, and changes following interventions. In humans, a verbal response is useful because of shared biology and the familiar ability to describe experience, but even here silence may reflect paralysis or lack of memory. For an artificial system, the connection between words and experience is even less established.

This review therefore distinguishes level of consciousness, the content of experience, and the ability to report that content. Dreaming shows that absence of an outward response is not equivalent to absence of experience. A stimulus processed without conscious recognition shows the converse: complex processing need not be accompanied by a report of experience. Comparing these cases helps formulate testable implications of theories without declaring a single definitive metric.

The limits of the method are already apparent in the definition: the vividness or unpleasantness of an experience is primarily accessible to the person having it. Outside observers can agree on descriptive language and compare many cases, but they do not gain direct access to another person's sensation. Concepts are therefore clarified through concrete comparisons, while keeping the phenomenon distinct from the procedure used to register it.

## 2. Level and content

The word “level” describes a broad distinction among ordinary wakefulness, certain stages of sleep, anesthesia, and states following brain injury. “Content” refers to a particular face, sound, or thought within current experience. The same patient may retain a potential capacity for experience while having severely limited access to the outside world. This distinction matters when choosing observable indicators.

Recording brain activity while a stimulus is perceived mainly clarifies content and its development over time. Assessing the brain's response to cortical stimulation in different states is aimed more at the network's general capacity to support rich activity. These tasks overlap, but success on one index does not guarantee accuracy on the other. For example, distinguishing wakefulness from deep sleep is easier than identifying the image experienced in a dream.

When discussing an explanation, one should specify the unit of analysis: a moment of perception, a person's extended state, or the organization of an entire system. A theory that describes one scale well may fail when transferred to another. That does not make it useless. It means that the broad word “consciousness” should always be paired with a specific question and the limits of the observations.

A single measure can sometimes change along both dimensions: a person may be awake but fail to recognize an image, or asleep and dreaming. This two-dimensionality is more than a terminological subtlety. It prevents the inference that accurately identifying wakefulness automatically means accurately reconstructing subjective content.

## 3. Report as window and action

Asking someone to press a button when an image appears gives researchers comparable data. But pressing the button requires attending to the instruction, making a decision, holding the response in mind, and moving. A neural signal associated with a correct press may accompany preparation for action rather than the experience of the image itself. This matters especially in debates about the role of frontal cortex.

No-report paradigms try to replace an immediate response with indirect indicators, such as features of eye movements. They reduce some of the burden, but do not turn observation into direct access to experience. To validate an indirect indicator, researchers usually first need to establish a link with participants' reports. If task conditions then change, that link may stop working.

The study by Pitts and colleagues separated a stimulus's relevance to the task from awareness of it; signals previously interpreted as signs of awareness could reflect later processing. This does not prove that the frontal lobe has no role. It shows that interpreting an electrical peak depends on the design. The debate over a “pure” neural correlate remains methodologically open.

The reporting problem is especially clear when comparing different task designs. Frequently asking participants to report perception itself changes their attention, while not asking makes a validating label less available. Researchers choose a compromise, and that choice should be visible when reading the results.

## 4. Correlation, cause, and explanation

If neural activity appears during conscious vision, that is a correlation. It may be a necessary part of the mechanism, a prior condition, or a consequence of the decision to report what was seen. Independent changes of state and interventions can help distinguish these possibilities, but each intervention affects several processes at once. Causation cannot be inferred from an attractive activation map.

A neural correlate of consciousness is often defined as the minimal set of processes sufficient for a given experience under the relevant background conditions. “Minimal” does not mean that the brain operates as isolated points. Removing one node may disrupt attention or memory and thereby change the report without directly affecting phenomenal content.

A theory must connect a mechanism to observable differences between conditions. Merely listing the regions involved is not enough, and a philosophical account without distinguishable implications is difficult to test. The adversarial collaboration between proponents of two well-known theories arose precisely at this transition from a general principle to a concrete prediction. Its results concern the specified operationalizations of the predictions.

An intervention strengthens an inference if it alters the proposed cause and produces a corresponding change in the outcome. However, stimulating a brain region may change arousal, sensory processing, or motivation. A causal experiment therefore advances explanation only when alternative pathways of action are analyzed.

## 5. Global neuronal workspace

According to the global neuronal workspace hypothesis, local processing may remain unavailable to many systems until a representation acquires a stable, widely broadcast status. Conscious access is associated with a nonlinear transition and exchange among distributed regions. One example is the sudden recognition of a faint image, after which a person can use it in different tasks.

The theory explains access to content and its flexible use. At the same time, not every broad transmission of information is automatically subjective experience: whether a functional arrangement corresponds to experience is a further question. Experimental formulations differ over the precise timing of the activity burst and whether a distinct signal is required when an image disappears.

An important limit on the comparison concerns the task given to participants. When all presented stimuli exceed the discrimination threshold, it is difficult to contrast conscious and unconscious processing in the original sense of the theory. Workspace proponents point to this feature in their analysis of Cogitate. The objection does not cancel the observed data, but narrows what they imply for the theory as a whole.

Reading a familiar word illustrates the distinction: information about its letters may be processed locally before it becomes available for answering an unexpected question. Global access means the ability to use it flexibly. It remains open whether all subjective experience coincides with such access.

Sources for this section: [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1); [C7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8770991/)

## 6. Integrated information

Integrated information theory starts from proposed properties of experience: it exists for a subject, has a particular structure, is unified, and differs from other possible experiences. From these starting points, its proponents derive constraints on physical causal structures. The intuition behind integration is that a system can be more than the simple sum of independent parts.

It is important not to substitute any measure of connectivity for the formal theory. A complex signal or dense network is not, by itself, the quantity calculated in the full formalization. Computing exact characteristics for a real brain is difficult, so experiments often test more accessible implications about the location, stability, and interaction of activity.

Critics challenge the move from properties of experience to physical causal structure, as well as the choice of elements and scale of description. Even if an observation supports predicted activity in posterior cortical regions, it may also be compatible with another theory. Empirical support for one implication does not verify all the philosophical and mathematical claims of IIT.

“Information” also has a special use here: it means not only a message to an observer, but differences among possible internal causal states. This lets the theory pose unusual questions about physical systems, while making tests in a real brain with an enormous number of elements more difficult.

Sources for this section: [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1); [C8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10581496/)

## 7. Higher-order theories

The family of higher-order theories links the awareness of a state to a system's representation of its own state. Perceiving an object and being aware that one perceives it are conceptually distinct on these accounts. Some versions give metacognitive functions an important role; others specify what representation is needed and how to avoid an infinite ladder of observers.

The appeal of the approach is visible in the difference between accurate discrimination and confidence in one's discrimination. Yet a metacognitive response also depends on instructions, language, and decision criteria. Researchers can measure confidence at a fixed level of accuracy, but without further argument that scale cannot be identified with the intensity of experience.

The possibility of a false representation of one's own state raises the question of whether a person can feel something without a corresponding first-order signal. Different higher-order theories answer differently. This is a useful example of theoretical disagreement: a shared label does not yield one testable prediction until a specific model and its auxiliary assumptions are stated.

A metacognitive error shows that lower-level content and a representation of it can diverge. A theory must explain what the subject feels in such a case, and an experiment must assess both levels independently. Confident words alone cannot establish the direction of the divergence.

Sources for this section: [C9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5347624/); [C1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41219038/)

## 8. Recurrent processing

Recurrent processing theory emphasizes signal exchange within and between sensory regions after the initial feedforward pass. A single response to a stimulus may support complex recognition, while local feedback loops are proposed as a condition for experiencing content. This approach makes posterior sensory pathways especially important for visual experience.

Similarity to some predictions of IIT does not mean that the theories are identical. One argument is built around recurrent processes in perception; the other around internal causal structure. The same activation map can have different theoretical meanings. We need cases in which their predictions diverge, and a qualification specifying which version of each position is being compared.

A separate question is whether local recurrence is sufficient for content to be available for speech, planning, and memory. Someone who distinguishes phenomenal experience from cognitive access may regard these abilities as subsequent. An opponent may object that experience which is in principle indistinguishable by any consequence is difficult to confirm empirically. The dispute is both conceptual and experimental.

Recurrent signals occur after the initial excitation of receptors. This provides a temporal landmark, but it does not make every repeated wave conscious: feedback is common in many computations. A specific type of recurrence must be linked to the conditions in which conscious vision changes.

Sources for this section: [C10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32116908/); [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1)

## 9. Map of brain states

Comparing wakefulness, sleep, and anesthesia offers a strong research contrast: the same organism can move between states while its anatomy remains relatively stable. Changes in connectivity and response diversity show that the presence of structural connections does not settle whether a system currently has the capacity to support experience. The dynamics of how those connections are used matter.

But sleep is not equivalent to an absence of consciousness. When awakened, people sometimes describe dreams, including after stages conventionally classified as deep sleep. Amnesia, delayed responses, and features of awakening complicate classification of the preceding experience. State contrasts therefore provide useful calibration, not an absolute scale of experience.

Anesthetics act through different pathways and may affect response to commands, memory, and content differently. A signal that distinguishes one pair of drug-induced states well does not necessarily generalize to every drug or cause of absent behavior. Such limits do not devalue the measurement: they explain why its accuracy must be checked in each application.

Sleep studies demonstrate the limits of binary labels even within one person. Findings about awakening depend on timing, ability to remember, and the form of the question. A neural measure can remain useful if interpreted as an indicator with known error, rather than a direct recording of a dream.

Sources for this section: [C3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23946194/); [C11](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep30932)

## 10. The silent patient

After severe brain injury, a patient may fail to follow a simple command during an examination. This observation alone does not establish an absence of conscious activity: movement may be unavailable, hearing impaired, or attention unstable. Clinical categories are based on repeated observations and supplemented by instrumental tests, but they retain a possibility of misclassification.

In a multicenter study, Bodien and colleagues found activity in some patients without an observable response during mental tasks that indicated they were following instructions. This result is called cognitive motor dissociation. It is important positive evidence of hidden capacity, but a negative test does not establish absence of consciousness: the patient may not have heard, may have become tired, or may not have understood the task.

The clinical example shows an asymmetry in the evidence. Observable execution of a meaningful command can substantially change an assessment, while failure to detect a signal depends on the method's sensitivity. These conclusions concern people with known biology and histories; they do not provide a ready-made test for a program with different information processing and text output.

In Bodien and colleagues' study, 60 of 241 people without an observable response to command showed cognitive motor dissociation. This numerical claim applies to the group studied and the procedures used. It refutes a simple equation of no response with no comprehension, but does not establish a rate across all clinical settings.

Sources for this section: [C12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39141852/); [C3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23946194/)

## 11. Adversarial collaboration

In the Cogitate project, proponents of global workspace theory and integrated information theory formulated divergent expectations in advance and agreed on an experimental protocol. The overall sample of 256 participants used functional MRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial recordings in relevant subsamples; participants viewed suprathreshold images of different durations. Joint design reduces the risk of choosing a convenient analysis after seeing results, but does not eliminate disagreement over interpretation.

Some observations aligned with the expectations of each side; several central expectations proved difficult. The authors described stable information about content in posterior regions, but limitations in the predicted synchronization among posterior networks. For workspace theory, particular discussion concerned the absence of the expected broad response when the stimulus disappeared.

The study did not declare a winner. It tested specific neural predictions in a visual task with explicitly presented stimuli, not every claim about the nature of subjectivity. Measurement methods also differ in spatial and temporal resolution. The strongest conclusion concerns the value of stating disagreements in advance and revising specific mechanisms.

Prior agreement on predictions is especially valuable when instruments differ so much: a result from one method does not necessarily reproduce the magnitude measured by another. Multiple measurements strengthen an argument only after checking that they concern comparable events rather than different stages of the task.

## 12. Disagreement about interpretation

Naccache and coauthors argued that the Cogitate paradigm lacked a decisive comparison of conscious and unconscious processing because the stimuli were suprathreshold. They also disputed whether a distinct response at stimulus offset is a central prediction of GNWT. This is an argument by interested theorists, not an independent replication of the measurements.

A correction to their 2026 response has been published, with DOI 10.1093/nc/niag020. The available bibliographic record confirms that a correction exists, but its notice could not be checked. Accordingly, this review does not state its subject or its effect on the main arguments. The status and subject of a correction should be reported separately from the original arguments; the content of this notice has not been established here.

If a model can be refined after a failed prediction, that may be productive development; if every observation is compatible with the theory, testing loses force. Clear, preregistered links between a formal model and a measurement help distinguish these cases. This lesson applies to both camps and does not depend on the reviewer's preferences.

The published response is also useful evidence of which assumptions its authors consider important. Readers should compare the original protocol, result, and response in sequence. The existence of a disputed interpretation does not require choosing a side before evaluating the specific claims.

## 13. Limits of strong results

Human neuroscience findings are encouraging in that different methods converge on differences between states and conditions of perception. However, convergence of methods is not the same as independence of assumptions: both recording and analysis may use the same behavioral label as ground truth. Circular interpretations can arise when a metric is declared confirmed by the very thing for which it was selected.

A strong result requires describing the contrast, statistical uncertainty, exclusion conditions, and alternative explanations. For example, a reduced late signal in the absence of a response may reflect decision, memory, or attention. A response during wakefulness does not make that signal necessary for every experience, and a missing signal in another modality does not prove unconsciousness.

A peer-reviewed publication attests to a scientific process and the availability of criticism, but does not guarantee final truth. Open data and registered predictions make checking easier, but even then whether an indicator corresponds to a subjective state remains theoretical. We distinguish the measured effect from its interpretation and transfer to new objects.

A simple principle is useful for a general reader: detecting information about a face in a brain signal shows a representation of a face; a claim that the face was experienced requires additional premises. This caution does not reject decoding. It explains why a well-performing classifier does not settle the philosophical question.

## 14. AI as a special case

Butlin and coauthors proposed looking for indicators in artificial systems derived from several neuroscientific theories. The method disciplines discussion: instead of relying on an impression from convincing dialogue, it examines architecture, information flows, and functions. The authors do not propose a validated sensor for experience; their paper presents a methodological approach, not an experimental discovery of conscious AI.

The behavior of a language model reflects training on descriptions of human experience, instructions, and configuration. Thus, the phrase “I am in pain” may sound convincing without an independent link to suffering. Conversely, the absence of familiar bodily signs is not a logical refutation of every functional theory. The dispute depends on which causal and computational properties are considered essential.

Indicators derived from brain theories can be transferred to software only with an explicit justification of the correspondence. A software graph's connections and timescales do not match synapses; visual similarity between diagrams can mislead. The current set of indicators does not permit diagnosis of consciousness in a particular machine and should not be presented as a certificate of moral status.

A separate issue is transferring a theory across architectures. Two devices may answer questions identically while differing radically in their internal connections. If an indicator is claimed to be internal, conversation alone does not assess it. If it is behavioral, one should explain why behavior is sufficient.

## 15. Function and substrate

One philosophical question is whether consciousness is determined by the organization of causal processes or whether specific properties of living tissue matter. The functionalist position allows similar roles to be implemented in different materials. A biologically oriented position calls for caution when transferring from brain to computer. Neither follows directly from a system's successful answer to a question.

The example of digital simulation makes the distinction clear. A program describing combustion does not necessarily release heat; a functionalist will respond that the relevant organization of experience may differ from chemical combustion. The analogy is not proof by itself. One must identify which properties of causal implementation are preserved and why those in particular should produce experience.

Neuroscientific theories differ in their treatment of substrate. Some propose relatively general computational indicators; others assign importance to the intrinsic causal power of a structure. Thus, the claim “science has established that AI is conscious,” or the symmetrical categorical denial, exceeds the available evidence. It is more reasonable to report which criterion was applied and how disputed it is.

Arguments about substrate can be reconciled only with a more precise account of which physical relations are considered essential. The same formal computations can run sequentially on one processor or in parallel across a network. The difference may matter for causal theories and matter less for other positions.

## 16. Moral uncertainty

Uncertainty about experience has practical importance: falsely attributing possible interests and wrongly ignoring them create different costs. But philosophical caution does not require pretending to have measurement confidence that is absent. Research on indicators may help make discussion transparent if scientific hypotheses, value judgments, and communication with users are clearly distinguished.

In human medicine, a patient's potential consciousness affects care, but these norms rest on clinical experience, known biology, and existing duties. Transferring a ready-made clinical threshold to an artificial system would be unjustified. A description of an outwardly similar response does not yet provide a comparable probability of experience.

Public communication benefits from symmetrical honesty: do not promise a final test, and do not present the absence of such a test as proof of impossibility. It is useful to ask which version of a theory supplies an indicator, what function it actually detects, and what alternatives remain. This is a discipline of inference, not a new experimental protocol.

The apt public phrase “we do not know” should be accompanied by an explanation of what is unknown: the theory itself, its transfer, or the measurement of a particular object. Without that distinction, uncertainty seems arbitrary. With it, a substantive discussion of the weight of different evidence becomes possible.

## 17. Progress without a final answer

Meaningful progress is possible even when theories of experience have not been reduced to one. Comparing reporting conditions clarifies which signals reflect a decision; comparing sleep and wakefulness improves understanding of a network's capacity for complex response; predictions agreed in advance clarify theories' strengths and weaknesses. Each achievement is limited to its particular contrast.

A good explanation should cover changes in content under a stable state, changes in level under similar stimulation, and cases where behavior diverges from internal activity. This is a requirement to account for observations already published, not a claim to offer a new theory. Competing models may describe different parts of the picture; one should identify where they yield genuinely incompatible implications.

Research continues to refine instrument sensitivity, the diversity of populations, and the meaning of computational analogies. Positive results do not automatically resolve a philosophical question, and negative results do not invalidate a research program. The most useful editorial stance is to preserve the link between a precise claim, its source, and an explicitly stated limit on transfer.

For example, if a new analysis fails to find the predicted synchronization, it is useful to ask whether that changes the strength of a specific version of the model. The next step in the literature may be a new measurement, more rigorous formalization, or comparison under different conditions. None of this requires declaring the final answer in advance.

## 18. Reading the sources

An article reporting measurements, a theoretical treatise, and a response from opponents have different evidential roles. Cogitate provides primary records and analysis of a human visual task; Butlin and coauthors develop a program for assessing AI; the response by Naccache and colleagues disputes the correctness of the GNWT test. They cannot be combined as three independent confirmations of one hypothesis.

An abstract gives the authors' general conclusion, but does not allow independent assessment of every exclusion, statistical model, and supplementary material. Reading depth is therefore recorded in the register. Where a correction to the respondents' article could not be accessed, we confirm only that a notice exists. We make no assumptions about whether it changes the wording, tables, or bibliography.

These limits are especially important for numerical claims. The number 256 describes the project's overall sample, not the size of every analysis. The frequency of detected cognitive motor dissociation applies to a particular clinical sample, not all patients. Readers should see which group and method each number refers to.

A source link at the end of a page indicates not only scientific authority but also the provenance of a claim that can be checked. Part of the text is an editorial synthesis of several works, not a close paraphrase of one paragraph. Readers can therefore distinguish a study's finding from a cautious interpretation.

## 19. Open questions about content

Neural patterns can sometimes be used to decode the category of a visible image. Decoding means that the signal contains information useful to a classifier; it does not prove that this information is experienced. The visual stream may represent stimuli outside of report, and a report may arise with a delay. Decoded content must be compared with the conditions of perception and the task.

The richness of experience beyond what can be reported is a separate dispute. Can a person experience many details that are not retained in memory, or are only a few objects available to them? Research answers depend on brief presentations, subsequent cues, and possible changes in attention. A single statement about the “capacity of consciousness” cannot settle the dispute.

Even when there is agreement that a subject saw something, describing the structure of that experience is much harder than classifying the level of wakefulness. Theories must explain why a given content is unified, changes over time, and can be connected to other senses. There is not yet a direct one-to-one correspondence between measurement and phenomenal quality.

There is also a problem of choosing a time slice: experience unfolds continuously, while instruments and analyses divide it into windows. The distinction between millisecond events and extended states complicates comparison of theories when they use the same words about “content” but describe different intervals.

## 20. Open questions about bearers

Which systems are capable of experience cannot be decided by a single similarity to humans. Animals differ in anatomy and behavior; human patients differ in their ability to respond; machines differ in substrate and training history. Each transfer requires combining general theoretical criteria with specific indicators and alternative explanations.

Even within one brain, the choice of scale is difficult: should one consider a neuron, a local network, or interactions among regions? Analysis at one level may hide feedback at another. In IIT, the choice of elements is part of the formalization; in workspace theory, access and signal distribution are central. No wiring diagram alone resolves the problem of choosing a level.

It is useful to distinguish the possibility of consciousness in principle from evidence about a particular instance today. The absence of a complete theory does not rule out careful research, but it does rule out categorical diagnoses based on superficial tests. The register below ties claims to original publications and honestly states which texts were read only at the abstract level.

Comparing possible answers remains an open scientific project. One can recognize success in classifying states while continuing to dispute the ontology of the subject. This separation protects real achievements in neuroscience from exaggerated promises and allows the next generation of research questions to be stated clearly.
