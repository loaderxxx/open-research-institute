## 1. Theory as a set of levels

Discussion of a theory of consciousness begins not with its name, but with distinctions among its starting claims, mathematical form, neural implementation, and experimental indicator. If one indicator is absent, this may refute a proposed implementation, but not automatically the philosophical premise. Conversely, agreement between an indicator and a prediction does not prove the entire chain of inference.

Comparing IIT and GNWT is especially difficult because one program begins from properties of experience and causal structure, while the other explains the availability of information to many functions. They therefore partly address different explananda. Yet both programs formulate neural expectations and can consequently be confronted with data.

The degree of certainty at each transition should be recorded. “The theory predicts” can be shorthand for a long sequence of assumptions about the task, time window, and instrument. When this sequence is published in advance, subsequent discussion becomes more testable. That was the aim of the adversarial collaboration protocol.

For example, stability of neural information is an indicator, not an axiom of IIT. It is important to ask which processes were supposed to realize that stability and why an unmeasured connection would truly affect the model. Likewise, a broad GNWT signal is not always equivalent to a response at every stimulus boundary. Levels of explanation help avoid an artificial victory for one theory over another. Publishing the agreed-upon bridges in advance gives independent readers a criterion for evaluation.

## 2. IIT's phenomenological starting points

IIT starts from the propositions that experience exists for the subject, has a specific content structure, is unified and definite, and excludes alternatives at a given moment. These are starting points of the theory, not results of an experiment on neurons. Requirements are then introduced for a physical mechanism that could have the corresponding intrinsic causal structure.

Integration in the ordinary sense is insufficient: many interconnected systems perform complex work. In the formalism, what matters is counterfactual causal relations among elements and their own possible states. Dynamic correlation between two measured regions only bears indirectly on these relations; a common external input can also produce correlation.

Measuring the exact theoretical quantity in a biological brain is practically difficult. Researchers therefore work with more accessible implications, such as the stability of information about content in posterior regions and interaction among them. Failure of such an implication is serious for the specific bridge from formula to data, but the fate of the theory's foundations requires a separate argument.

Phenomenal axioms are not derived from the distribution of activation across brain regions. An experiment can assess the implications of a proposed correspondence, not the self-evidence of its starting point. That is why philosophical debate about whether the listed properties are necessary is not closed by favorable neuroimaging. At the same time, formalization makes the requirements more precise than the vague claim that “the brain integrates information.” This strength remains even when particular predictions are criticized.

Sources for this section: [C8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10581496/)

## 3. Choosing a mechanism and scale

Causal analysis in IIT depends on which elements of a system are treated as basic and which time steps are chosen. Changing the scale can change the resulting structure. In a biological brain, cells, groups of cells, and regions are intertwined; in an artificial system, a software graph differs from the physical organization of memory and processor.

This raises critical questions about whether a “main” complex can be uniquely identified and how it should be compared with experience. The familiar number of connections does not settle the issue: strong integration in one description does not guarantee the required causal properties in another. This is a fundamental limit on simple popular interpretations of the theory.

The empirical debate about where information about a visual object is retained only partly concerns the broader choice of bearer. Support for posterior cortex is consistent with some expectations of IIT, but other theories also assign it a role. A discriminating test requires the specific consequences on which the positions diverged in advance.

If one physical process is described at different levels, there should be rules for choosing a level, not only an observer's intuition. Without them, evaluating a machine may depend on an arbitrary block diagram. IIT authors propose formal conditions, but their computational cost and philosophical plausibility are debated. An experiment on posterior cortex does not replace this analysis. It tests a specific hypothesis about a human bearer selected for comparison.

## 4. The GNWT principle

Global neuronal workspace theory describes a network in which a selected representation becomes available to many specialized processes. Local processing may occur without conscious access; strong information, or information sustained by attention, is broadly broadcast and persists long enough for flexible use.

The concept of “ignition” denotes nonlinear dynamics of access. It does not mean that every peak at a frontal electrode in any task necessarily constitutes consciousness. Different versions of the model impose different requirements on the timing of activation, maintenance, and termination of a representation. These should be checked against the specific text of the authors.

Especially informative is comparing the same content under conscious and unconscious processing, where input properties and access state can be separated. If input is always explicitly visible, the data clarify the organization of processing for conscious stimuli but test the original contrast less strongly. This is the core of the later criticism of the Cogitate project.

A workspace can be understood as the ability of one representation to affect several independent kinds of processing. But a functional description does not specify the minimum number of recipients needed to count as global. Studies use patterns of distributed activity and dynamics; the specific threshold remains a modeling question. A plausible broadcasting metaphor must therefore give way to prespecified measurable conditions. This transition is both the strength and the difficulty of GNWT.

Sources for this section: [C7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8770991/); [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1)

## 5. Frontal and posterior regions

The contrast “frontal or posterior cortex” is convenient for a headline but simplifies neural dynamics. Posterior sensory regions carry detailed information about content; frontal networks participate in tasks, rules, decisions, and information transfer. The key question is which signals are necessary for experience, which reflect access, and which accompany a required report.

Recording activity does not establish that a region is necessary. If a participant has to respond, regions associated with the response predictably become active. In a task without an immediate response, they may be less prominent. This allows both an interpretation in terms of a post-perceptual signal and the possibility of another, subtler form of broad exchange.

Cogitate's predictions specifically concerned temporal and spatial features of network interaction. Evaluating them is stronger than drawing the general conclusion that one half of the brain “contains” consciousness. Methodological selectivity, recording limits, and versions of the theory should accompany every anatomical claim.

The distinction between “contains” and “uses” helps in reading the results: posterior regions may encode features of a face, while a frontal region uses those features to make a decision. If both activities occur during conscious vision, it remains to be determined which one pertains to experience. Data are needed that can disentangle use from seeing itself, and the indirect label of seeing is also imperfect. Simply listing the regions involved does not answer the question.

## 6. Protocol before results

In 2023, participants in the adversarial collaboration published a protocol comparing divergent expectations. Joint preparation matters because proponents of different theories recognize in advance what result would pose a problem for their position. In a retrospective dispute after measurements, it is always easy to select a favorable interpretation.

However, preregistration does not turn a theory into unified software code. Operationalization includes selection of stimuli, durations, methods, and analysis criteria, which may not cover every aspect of a theory. Results require reporting whether the plan was followed and explaining deviations; objectivity comes from inspectability, not merely from the word “pre-registered.”

The project used several measurement methods because spatial localization and temporal resolution differ. Agreement in direction across methods strengthens an inference, while disagreement prompts examination of which quantities were compared. The joint design is a model of organizational honesty amid unavoidable theoretical differences.

The protocol has historical value regardless of which camp would win the comparison. It articulates differences that were often hidden by broad theory names. This allows discussion of not only statistical significance but also how diagnostic a particular result is. If both models expect the same pattern in advance, finding that pattern does little to choose between them. Prespecified incompatible expectations are especially valuable.

Sources for this section: [C13](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0268577); [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1)

## 7. Cogitate task and sample

The 2025 publication reported an overall sample of 256 participants. They viewed explicitly presented visual images for different durations. Measurements included functional MRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial electroencephalography. The participant count applies to the project as a whole and should not be attributed to every subanalysis without checking.

Different durations allowed researchers to distinguish brief responses to stimulus onset and offset from information sustained about a stimulus. They examined both local representation of a category and interactions among regions. This tests human visual perception under specified conditions; it is not direct reading of experience from a signal.

The suprathreshold nature of the stimuli became important in later discussion. Even if visible content changes, the contrast between “seen” and “not seen” is limited here. The data are useful for testing structural and temporal predictions about conscious processing, but generalizing to all forms of unconscious processing would require separate grounds.

Functional MRI is sensitive to slow hemodynamic changes, MEG better tracks rapid magnetic signals, and intracranial electrodes cover only accessible sites. Their differences are therefore not three repeated measurements of one identical object. Using them together enables tests of spatial and temporal aspects, but interpreting the complex result requires comparing the relevant subsamples and conditions. The overall sample size does not replace this check.

Sources for this section: [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1)

## 8. What supported IIT

Researchers found a stable representation of information about consciously perceived content in posterior cortical regions. This is compatible with an important strand of IIT's predictions concerning a posterior complex. The result concerns decodable information in a specific visual task, not direct measurement of phenomenal quality.

Support requires comparison with competitors. Posterior cortex has long been known to participate in visual processing, and GNWT does not require its absence. A fact about local content is therefore not an exclusive victory. The strength of evidence depends on whether it was predicted as discriminating and how precisely.

Even a confident detection of information does not determine the theory's full causal structure. A decoder may use statistical association that is not equivalent to the intrinsic causal power of a complex. The justified conclusion is limited: the measurement is consistent with a specific neural implication, while the fundamental axioms require separate argument.

A posterior pattern may support a representation of an object throughout its presentation, which is an important fact about perception. But whether this pattern is necessary for experience requires further argument. It may provide input to another access mechanism. Thus, the result is informative and theoretically ambiguous at the same time. An honest account retains both points without reducing them to general skepticism.

Sources for this section: [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1)

## 9. What complicated IIT

The article reports the absence of some expected stable interactions among posterior regions that were supposed to support an integrated representation. This complicates the chosen experimental translation of an IIT prediction. The formulation of the expectation, measurement sensitivity, and distinction between measured functional connectivity and theoretical causal integration all matter.

A negative result is not proof that all possible connections are absent. One instrument and time window do not cover all brain dynamics. But a preregistered failure should not be dissolved into the claim that the measurement was irrelevant altogether. Its significance is determined by published interpretation rules and additional analyses.

The set of results creates a mixed picture: posterior content supports one side, while the expected interaction constrains the other. This outcome is more useful than a media announcement of a winner because it shows exactly where the theory needs a more precise bridge from causal structure to available recordings.

The prespecified form of integration is especially important: connectivity measured after choosing a time window may differ from the connection theorists meant before the experiment. One should not retroactively declare every new measure equivalent to the one that failed. But observed synchronization should also not be equated with IIT's complete intrinsic causal structure. Clarifying the correspondence between them is a distinct result of the scientific debate.

Sources for this section: [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1)
## 10. What supported GNWT

The authors found information about content in visual, ventrotemporal, and inferior frontal regions, as well as specific synchronization between frontal and early visual regions; this is consistent with some GNWT expectations, but does not by itself establish full global broadcasting. The word “broad” requires precise specification of regions and timing. Early sensory spread, a later decision, and sustained access are not the same mechanism.

GNWT does not deny the importance of posterior content; the proposal is that specialized regions supply material for global use. The predominance of posterior information alone is therefore not a direct refutation. The sharper question is when and under what conditions the expected broad communication is observed.

Results cannot be reduced to a binary assessment when different predictions are being tested. Partial support should be called partial, and revisions to admissible forms of “ignition” should be distinguished from predictions fixed before the results. This allows progress to be assessed without turning a theory into an immovable slogan.

If a globally available representation is supposed to support flexible response, it is important to test which part of the signal is due to expecting a response and which to the opportunity to choose tasks. In a suprathreshold experiment, the participant already sees the stimulus, and observed spread may result from several processes. This does not make the support empty, but reduces its specificity for consciousness. The quantitative strength of evidence depends on the prespecified contrast.

Sources for this section: [C2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1); [C7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8770991/)

## 11. What complicated GNWT

One observation under discussion is the absence of the predicted broad response synchronized with the image's disappearance. The study authors described this as a difficulty for a specific implementation of GNWT. Another issue concerns the localization and persistence of information: the predicted workspace dynamics did not fully match the measured pattern.

GNWT proponents disputed the importance of the offset response to the theory's core. Their response should be read alongside the original protocol and observations, not substituted for the results. If the prediction was agreed in advance, its mismatch remains a fact about it even if the broad theory can survive revision.

One must distinguish a lack of statistical confirmation for an expected signal from proof that such a mechanism never exists. Differences in instruments, conditions, and event definitions limit the inference. Most strongly, the study shows tension between published predictions and this experiment.

“Offset” refers to the image's disappearance and the start of the next processing period. A response to an event boundary may differ from sustained representation. When the agreed prediction concerns precisely that boundary, its failure should be reported separately from results about stimulus onset. One can then ask whether the failure concerns the core access mechanism or an auxiliary temporal model. These questions are compatible.

## 12. The GNWT proponents' response

Naccache and colleagues emphasized that theories of conscious processing should ideally be compared in tasks that include a conscious-unconscious contrast. In Cogitate, both kinds of visual stimuli selected for the primary comparison were consciously perceived; the results therefore say less about the threshold for information entering the workspace.

The authors also proposed a different status for the response at stimulus offset: it should not be treated as an essential part of the theory. This interpretive defense can be discussed against the preregistration text. It does not change the measured results, but it changes the weight given to a specific failure when evaluating GNWT as a whole.

A 2026 correction to the response was published; its notice was found, but its contents were not checked in the accessible reading. Conclusions here are therefore limited to the original main argument that was read. For further formal work, the corrected text should first be checked, without attributing unknown details to the notice.

The significance of the criticism also depends on which claim is being made about the experiment. It directly tested prespecified spatiotemporal expectations, not every possible unconscious condition. Pointing to the missing contrast limits a general inference about conscious availability, but does not erase the mismatch between the measurements and published expectations. The existence of a correction should be reflected in the bibliography without inventing its content. This keeps the debate accurate.

## 13. How to evaluate a theory's revision

Theories develop after experiments, and refinement by itself is not a violation of scientific method. A new distinction between access and signal maintenance may make a prediction more precise. But if a refinement is introduced only after seeing a result, it does not turn the original failure into a prediction confirmed in advance.

In practice, two ledgers should be kept: how well the data matched the originally stated model, and how well the revised version explains them together with prior observations. The first measures the strength of a particular test; the second, the productivity of an ongoing program. Both questions matter and should not be conflated.

This applies to IIT as well: if measured connectivity is said to be an incorrect substitute for intrinsic causal structure, it must be clearly stated which accessible indicator would correspond to that structure. Without such a bridge, it is impossible to distinguish genuine development of the formalism from a defense compatible with any outcome.

Openly acknowledging a failed prediction can improve the next version of a theory. It becomes a problem only when revision is not accompanied by new, risky implications. Equally, a critic should recognize that no finite experiment covers an entire program. The standard is mutual: unchanging claims require a rigorous translation into observation, and new interpretations must be distinguishable from old ones.

## 14. Other theories

Higher-order theories connect consciousness with representations of one's own states; recurrent-processing theory emphasizes sensory feedback. They remind us that the IIT-versus-GNWT dilemma does not exhaust the field. Some posterior-cortex findings may fit both alternatives, although their causal explanations differ.

Theories also differ on what exactly needs explaining: phenomenal content, access for report, or an organism's unified state. A successful working-memory model is not necessarily a theory of sensory quality, and a theory of quality may leave speech mechanisms unspecified. Direct comparison requires a shared set of dependent variables.

Adversarial work is useful because it forces verbal claims to be translated into observations that distinguish them. Future comparisons should be judged by the same standard of prespecified conditions, but this review does not propose its own experiment or algorithm. It only explains published advances and remaining limitations.

Alternatives show that one neural result often has more than two explanations. Local recurrent feedback may matter without IIT's formalism; metacognitive access may change alongside frontal activity without the whole apparatus of GNWT. A victory on one indicator therefore does not follow from a competitor's failure. A theory must predict the observation better than available alternatives, not merely appear compatible after publication.

## 15. The problem of reverse inference

If a frontal signal is detected alongside a report, the conclusion “it produced the experience” is reverse inference: reasoning from an observed consequence to one of several possible causes. The same is true of a posterior signal carrying content information. Since both regions perform other functions, task context and independent checks are necessary.

Separating report from vision reduces one confound, but an indirect indicator of vision itself needs validation. Separating attention from consciousness is complicated by the fact that attention changes the quality of sensory processing. Each experimental improvement relocates methodological difficulties; it does not automatically eliminate all of them.

The philosophical question of experience does not make neural data useless. It requires precision in phrasing: a signal covaries with a report, classifies a condition, changes under intervention, or is necessary for a specific kind of access. These claims have different strengths and different ways they can be falsified.

Reverse inference is especially vulnerable when one label serves both for a technical variable and for a phenomenon. “Information,” “access,” and “integration” may refer to mathematical statistics, physiological function, and experience. Moving between these meanings requires an explicit explanation. Otherwise, a dispute may look empirical even though the parties define the object of inquiry differently. Precision of language improves data analysis as well.

## 16. Sleep and anesthesia as tests

Contrasts among wakefulness, sleep, and anesthesia help determine whether a proposed network is accompanied by a preserved capacity for experience. The dynamics of responses to magnetic stimulation may change substantially with state. But such findings measure general capacity and do not name the specific content of a dream.

Dreams show that external immobility is not equivalent to unconsciousness. Anesthesia can dissociate response, memory, and experience. A model that predicts only the probability of a button press therefore does not explain the entire clinical picture. Theories should be compared with several kinds of data without collapsing one into another.

Transitions between states are also useful for separating anatomical possibility from current dynamics. Nevertheless, drugs, injuries, and sleep differ in mechanism. A universal neural indicator needs separate validation across states, and transfer to an artificial bearer is even more distant.

Differences among states can serve as an external test of a prediction originally derived from a visual task. But transfer requires considering other causes of brain change: analgesia, memory, motor ability, and duration of sleep. If a prediction works only where it was developed, its generality is weaker than it may appear. If it is stable across different conditions, that is important, though still indirect, support.

## 17. The clinical challenge

Patients who do not observably follow commands sometimes show characteristic responses to instructions in EEG or functional MRI. The multicenter work by Bodien and colleagues confirms the importance of cognitive motor dissociation in the studied sample. This is positive evidence of preserved functions despite limited motor output.

For theories that equate consciousness with report, such a case requires distinguishing internal access, intention, and outward movement. Theories of content must explain how internal states can persist when the channel of expression is impaired. No single test fully solves these theoretical problems.

A negative result on an imagery task depends on hearing, comprehension, fatigue, and analytical sensitivity. The data therefore warn against categorical inference from absent response. Clinical reasoning operates within human neurobiology and does not transfer automatically to programs.

Clinical observations require consideration of “covert” access without behavior. Even a preserved ability to follow an instruction mentally is not a precise map of all the patient's experiences. Conversely, inability to perform an abstract task does not mean there is no pain or visual imagery. Theories must distinguish minimal content from complex cognitive function. This limits excessive claims based on one family of command-following tests.

## 18. Statistics and replication

A large overall sample and several modalities improve reliability, but do not mean that every individual comparison is equally large. Analysis is distributed across instruments, participants, and measures. For a specific claim, the relevant dataset and criterion should be checked rather than citing only the number 256.

Negative findings depend on power, recording quality, and the prespecified minimum effect; positive findings require assessment of multiple comparisons and independent confirmation. Preregistration reduces freedom in later interpretation but does not eliminate technical decisions and deviations. Replication in another sample has separate value.

Open project datasets help with new analyses, although reanalyzing the same recordings is not an independent replication. Meta-analysis also cannot fix incompatible definitions of a conscious condition without conceptual work. Methodological transparency and precision in theoretical predictions complement one another.

Uncertainty intervals and the exact structure of a sample matter no less than a striking mean effect. When comparing laboratories, some differences may arise from signal processing and exclusion criteria. Reanalysis of open data tests robustness to analytic choices; independent replication tests robustness to new recordings. These procedures answer different questions, and both matter for theory.

## 19. A cautious synthesis

Cogitate found support and difficulties for individual predictions of both positions. Support for posterior representation does not prove all of IIT; absence of the expected response at stimulus offset does not close the case for GNWT. Both assessments become substantive only when the link between a general principle, a model, and the measured event is clear.

The GNWT proponents' response highlighted the importance of a conscious-unconscious processing contrast; the independence of this objection from the unknown contents of the later correction should be checked before citing the updated article. This edition records publication of the notice without speculating about its effect.

The project's scientific value also lies in competing parties agreeing to state possible problems before seeing the data. A later reader can assess which expectations survived testing and which are being revised. The discussion remains open and concerns human visual neuroscience, not diagnosis of machine consciousness.

The overall assessment is not a vote for the more attractive metaphor. It depends on the precision of formal claims, the advance riskiness of the prediction, measurement quality, and competing explanations. The results currently open specific tasks for refining neural mechanisms rather than offering a final formula for subjectivity. In particular, a human experiment should not be transferred to digital systems: it has no calibration for such an inference.

## 20. Remaining questions for comparison

The published adversarial comparison raises the next question: exactly how are formal principles linked to recordable features of the human brain? When refining a theory, one should distinguish a change in the initial premise from a change in an auxiliary measurement assumption. Only then can a new test say more than the previous one.

Another problem is the relationship between content and level. A visual task studies what a person sees, while sleep and anesthesia change the general capacity to experience anything. A theory convincing for one measure may not explain the other. Different states and tasks provide complementary constraints.

Finally, neural results do not automatically answer questions about animals and machines. Inference from human cortex requires a justified causal analogy and separate calibration of an indicator. Today's data most strongly refine human neuroscience of consciousness, while leaving questions about subjectivity in other bearers open.

A substantive comparative conclusion should specify the temporal and spatial level at which an expectation was formulated. Success at one level does not automatically transfer to another. It is especially important to distinguish observation of a posterior visual representation from a claim about intrinsic causal structure, and broad exchange from a specific peak at stimulus offset. Such precision allows new results to be evaluated without retroactively changing the earlier test. The question of machine experience requires its own justification.
