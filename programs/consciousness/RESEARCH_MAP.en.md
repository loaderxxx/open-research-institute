## Three orientation books

| Book | Why it helps; access |
|---|---|
| David J. Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory* (1996), [author page](https://consc.net/the-conscious-mind/) | Frames phenomenal consciousness, the explanatory gap, and strong AI. Published by Oxford University Press; the author page provides the contents and introduction, while the full book is generally paid or library access. It is an argument, not empirical confirmation. |
| Anil Seth, *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness* (2021), [publisher page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/566315/being-you-by-anil-seth/) | An accessible account of predictive perception, the embodied self, and consciousness neuroscience, with examples from the author's research. The publisher record and sample are open; the full book is paid or available through libraries. It presents one scientific interpretation. |
| Christof Koch, *The Feeling of Life Itself: Why Consciousness Is Widespread but Can't Be Computed* (2019), [MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262539555/the-feeling-of-life-itself/) | Introduces IIT and the dispute over which physical systems could have experience; read alongside critics and empirical tests. The publisher page is open; the book is paid or library access. It defends a theory rather than serving as a neutral textbook. |

## Research and open resources

| Work | Question and result | Limits and access |
|---|---|---|
| Cogitate Consortium, Ferrante, O., Gorska-Klimowska, U. et al., [“Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1), *Nature* 642, 133–142 (2025), DOI: [10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1) | A preregistered, direct comparison of predictions from two theories in 256 people found partial support and problems for specific predictions from both. | Open article. It tests selected predictions in visual tasks; it neither settles the nature of experience nor exhausts the theories' variants. |
| Butlin et al., [“Identifying indicators of consciousness in AI systems”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1364661325002864), *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* (2026), DOI: [10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011) | Proposes deriving testable indicators from neuroscience theories and using them to assess the likelihood of consciousness in particular systems. | Open under a Creative Commons licence. This is a methodological framework, not a test that has demonstrated experience in AI. Indicators depend on contested theoretical assumptions. |
| Toker et al., [“Adversarial AI reveals mechanisms and treatments for disorders of consciousness”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02220-4), *Nature Neuroscience* (2026), DOI: [10.1038/s41593-026-02220-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-026-02220-4) | Combines neural networks with interpretable neural-field models; the authors report tests of several predictions about disorders of consciousness and possible stimulation. | Open article. The models use large neurophysiology datasets, but mechanistic and treatment claims remain bounded by samples and model assumptions. This is not an indicator of AI consciousness. |
| Bariach et al., [“Seemingly conscious AI risks”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-026-01294-x), *AI and Ethics* (2026), DOI: [10.1007/s43681-026-01294-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-026-01294-x) | A review and expert survey organize behavioral cues that lead users to attribute consciousness to AI and the possible risks of that perception. | Full text is open. It studies human attribution and social risks, not systems' phenomenal states; the review framework and expert survey do not replace replication. |

## Three historical milestones

1. **1990 — neural mechanisms become a research program.** Crick and Koch proposed studying neural mechanisms of visual awareness, including attention, short-term memory, and synchronous oscillations ([“Towards a neurobiological theory of consciousness”](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/7fap3-5v118), *Seminars in the Neurosciences* 2, 263–275). This is a research program, not a completed theory of experience; no DOI is confirmed for this publication.
2. **2013 — PCI as a clinical indicator of brain-response complexity.** Casali et al. introduced the perturbational complexity index from TMS-EEG to assess consciousness independently of sensory processing and behavior ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23946194/); DOI [10.1126/scitranslmed.3006294](https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.3006294)). It measures a brain response under a defined procedure; it does not directly “read” experience.
3. **2025 — adversarial comparison of theories.** The Cogitate Consortium, Ferrante, and co-authors recorded competing predictions from IIT and GNWT and tested them in a shared protocol ([Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1)); subsequent replies continued debate about exactly what had been predicted.

## Active researchers and teams

- **Anil Seth and the Sussex Centre for Consciousness Science**, University of Sussex: the group studies neural and computational models of perception, selfhood, and consciousness ([centre profile](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/research/centres/sussex-centre-for-consciousness-science/people)). A recent specific work by Seth is a 2025 comment on standards for scientific debate about consciousness theories ([*Nature Neuroscience*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-025-01913-6)); in October 2026 he co-authored a public critical essay on AI consciousness ([*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03313-7)). The latter is a position piece, not an experiment.
- **Patrick Butlin, Tim Bayne, Stephen Fleming, Liad Mudrik, and co-authors**, an interdisciplinary group represented in publications by Eleos AI Research, Monash, UCL, Tel Aviv University, UCI, and other institutions: they develop theory-derived indicators and examine their limits. Recent work includes the open 2026 indicator paper and a short follow-up on mimicry and internal system variants ([2026 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2026.04.006); [Butlin's Eleos AI Research profile](https://eleosai.org/); authors and affiliations for the response at [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42036253/)). This is research methodology, not a positive diagnosis.
- **Martin M. Monti and an interdisciplinary team**, UCLA lab studying consciousness and cognition in coma ([lab profile](https://montilab.psych.ucla.edu/)): a recent paper models mechanisms of coma and proposes testable physiological hypotheses ([*Nature Neuroscience*, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02220-4)). The paper documents active work; its conclusions still need independent validation.

## Current work and open questions

In 2025–2026, researchers are testing neuroscience theories against one another, formalizing indicators for AI consciousness, and studying how people perceive conversational systems. The year 2026 brought both an open paper on risks from “seemingly conscious AI” and a theory-derived assessment method. These are distinct questions: behavior, internal functional properties, human attribution, and subjective experience cannot be treated as interchangeable. As of this check, no experiment has established that a particular current language model has subjective experience.

Open questions include which theory predictions are precise enough to distinguish experimentally; how to validate indicators in nonhuman systems; how to control for reporting, expectations, and model training to talk about itself; and how to separate social anthropomorphism risks from hypotheses about experience.

**Suggested next reading:** start with Butlin et al. (2026), then compare the specific predictions with the methods and results of the Cogitate Consortium adversarial test (2025), and read the replies for interpretive limits. For the clinical side, read Toker et al. (2026), keeping separate the inference about disorders of consciousness in patients from claims about AI.
