## Content and evidence

Mansuri and Rao review almost five hundred studies of participation and decentralisation. They distinguish organic civic mobilisation from participation induced by external programmes, concentrating on the latter. The inspected sections set local information against inequality, capture and the need for accountability. The review combines econometric research and cases, so its conclusions do not constitute the result of one overarching experiment.

## ORI critical analysis

The central methodological question is what participation actually means. Attending meetings, controlling budgets, jointly designing projects and being able to replace leaders confer different powers. Comparisons cannot combine these arrangements merely because they share a label. They should identify the concrete decision available to participants and the consequences of refusal.

Selection is equally important. Time, literacy and connections may affect both participation and benefits received. Comparing active and inactive residents without addressing these differences does not isolate the procedure’s effect. Even randomising invitations measures a particular invitation in particular conditions rather than communities’ universal capacity for self-government.

Average benefits can conceal changes in influence. ORI should separately describe service recipients, decision participants and those bearing costs. These groups need not coincide. Project durability also needs assessment after external finance and facilitation end.

## Programme relevance

The book helps resist romanticising local agreement and directs attention to supporting institutions. A useful next step would compare two projects with similar participation procedures but different opportunities for appeal and independent oversight. Such a comparison must account for resources, history and spillovers. This is ORI’s research proposal; the review does not supply a ready-made algorithm for every community.
