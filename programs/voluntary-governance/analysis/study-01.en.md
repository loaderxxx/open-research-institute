## Content and type of contribution

The article proposes a shared language for studying social-ecological systems so findings across disciplines can accumulate. Its abstract identifies ten subsystem variables affecting the likelihood of self-organisation for sustainable resource use. This is an analytical framework informed by existing research, rather than a single experiment or universal governance ranking.

## ORI critical analysis

A framework is useful first as a way to formulate questions. Without shared structure, researchers may call different things success: forest preservation, income growth or fewer disputes. ORI should define the unit of analysis, time horizon and observed outcome before comparing institutions.

A list of factors does not itself establish causation. Participants’ resource dependence may affect rule creation, but existing rules can also change dependence and group composition. Distinguishing these directions requires event sequences, comparable cases or another appropriate research design. Merely completing a table of variables cannot do that.

Scale is a separate difficulty. A group may preserve local sustainability while moving environmental costs beyond its boundary. Analysis of voluntary cooperation should therefore include spillovers and representation of affected nonparticipants. Agreement inside a system does not adequately establish its overall value.

## Programme relevance

The paper should support comparable descriptions while preserving the distinction between classification and hypothesis testing. Further reading needs to recover variable definitions, relationships and limits of applicability. For ORI, a particularly important question is which features can be measured before cooperation emerges and which already reflect its consequences. This ordering helps avoid explaining successful self-government through indicators that simply restate its success.
