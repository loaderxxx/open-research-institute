## Question and evidence

The Indonesian road-project field experiment compares stronger government audits with citizen monitoring. Audits substantially reduced unexplained expenditure; the average citizen-monitoring effect was smaller. Randomisation supports claims about these interventions, not a general ranking of state and voluntary institutions.

## ORI critical analysis

For this programme, participation should not be reduced to expenditure control. Residents may value having a voice without measurable reductions in losses. Conversely, good financial performance does not establish that a procedure is fair or accessible. Representation and oversight objectives therefore need separate specification.

Monitoring involves a sequence: someone notices a problem, obtains confirmation, reports it and anticipates consequences. Failure at any transition can weaken participation’s influence. An open meeting does not guarantee access to technical information, and the ability to complain does not guarantee a response. Researchers should identify which part of this sequence the intervention changes.

Individual and collective motivation should also be distinguished. A user may understand a problem but expect a complaint to damage relations with influential neighbours. Opportunities to organise safely and the availability of real alternatives matter for explaining behaviour. These are ORI’s analytical questions, not additional experimental findings.

## Programme relevance

The study offers an example of testing a specific accountability mechanism against an independently measured outcome. Further work should examine costs, persistence and possible displacement of misconduct into other categories. Transfer to other voluntary communities requires a fresh description of authority, incentives and measurement. One setting can challenge an excessively broad promise about participation without exhausting all of participation’s functions.
