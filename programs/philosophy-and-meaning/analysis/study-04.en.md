## What the instrument measures

The paper introduces a ten-item questionnaire with separate presence-of-meaning and search-for-meaning scales. Its abstract reports three studies of internal consistency, temporal stability, factor structure and validity, including comparison with other instruments. The construct is perceived meaning in life. This is psychometric research rather than philosophical proof of particular goals’ value.

## ORI critical analysis

Separating presence from search matters: active searching need not indicate an absence of meaning. Someone may have meaningful relationships while revising future goals. Combining the scales into one overall score would require independent justification and could obscure distinct processes.

Validity should not be treated as a universal certificate. It concerns a particular interpretation of results under particular conditions. Strong internal consistency indicates related responses, but does not establish coverage of an entire concept. Stability is useful for stable characteristics, yet does not itself demonstrate sensitivity to change.

The limits of self-report are central to the programme. People describe their understanding through supplied wording and available categories. Cultural differences may reflect forms of expression as well as experience. Translation must test conceptual comparability rather than dictionary accuracy alone.

## Programme relevance

The MLQ can inform questions about people’s experience while leaving normative analysis independent. Before use, ORI should inspect the full method, scoring rules and evidence for the intended language and population. Correlation with well-being cannot establish a causal effect of searching for meaning or prescribe a particular life choice. Further reading should determine which interpretations of each scale the original validation actually supports.
