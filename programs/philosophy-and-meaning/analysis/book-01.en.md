## Aim of the book

Metz seeks what the conditions making lives meaningful have in common. The publisher abstract describes a critical comparison of theories by their ability to explain the significance of morality, inquiry and creativity. He defends an account linking meaning to intelligence directed towards fundamental conditions of human existence. This is normative philosophical investigation rather than measurement of a psychological state.

## ORI critical analysis

The first question concerns example selection. If a theory is assessed by how well it explains activities already considered meaningful, those initial judgments need justification. Otherwise agreement with the examples quietly becomes evidence for the theory itself. ORI should seek cases involving care, ordinary work and limited opportunities that might challenge the initial classification.

A second question distinguishes the source of value from knowledge of it. An objectivist may hold that desire alone does not create value, but still needs to explain how people recognise it and resolve reasonable disagreements. Expert authority cannot settle that question by itself.

Assessing activities comparatively should also be distinguished from ranking human dignity. Even if some actions confer more meaning, it does not follow that a person deserves less respect or fewer rights. That inference would require additional normative premises.

## Programme relevance

The book suits study of argumentative structure: thesis, grounds, counterexample and possible revision. Full reading should establish how fundamentality is defined and what limits apply to intelligent orientation. ORI can compare this position with subjectivist and hybrid accounts without converting a philosophical preference into an empirically validated scale. The practical result is clearer grounds for evaluation rather than a ready-made prescription for a meaningful life.
