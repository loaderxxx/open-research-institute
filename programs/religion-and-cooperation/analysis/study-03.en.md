## Question, method and result

The meta-analysis combines 701 effects across 237 samples. Its abstract reports a stronger association of religiosity with self-reported prosociality than with directly measured behaviour. The authors consider alternative explanations, including limitations of behavioural tasks and features of self-description. This is a quantitative synthesis of heterogeneous studies; aggregation does not turn the underlying correlations into causal effects.

## ORI critical analysis

The key reading lesson is that measurement forms part of the research question. Self-report can cover rare everyday actions absent from a short game. It also depends on memory, interpretation of norms and self-presentation. Behavioural tasks observe action directly, but within an imposed situation and restricted time window. Divergence therefore does not automatically establish hypocrisy or invalidate laboratory research.

Full reading should examine effect dependence, rules for combining prosocial and antisocial outcomes, publication-bias assessment and heterogeneity. Large participant numbers reduce some random error without removing shared systematic measurement problems. Different forms of religiosity also cannot be treated as interchangeable merely because they enter one synthesis.

## Programme relevance

ORI should construct a matrix specifying what was measured, by which method and towards whom. It should distinguish belief, practice and belonging, alongside assistance to ingroups and outgroups. Subsequent studies could compare self-report and behaviour within the same participants where appropriate to the question. Here the meta-analysis guides measurement scrutiny rather than providing a final numerical verdict on religious people’s morality. Its value lies partly in exposing why apparently similar claims may refer to different outcomes and deserve different interpretations.
