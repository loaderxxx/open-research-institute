## Content and form of argument

The book connects expanding cooperation among strangers with the spread of belief in watchful gods. Its publisher description recognises both cooperation within groups and potential conflict between them. Cooperation in secular societies forms another theme. This is an evolutionary explanatory framework combining several kinds of evidence, not one study isolating religion’s influence across human history.

## ORI critical analysis

Testing requires separating at least three propositions: belief changes particular behaviour; religious institutions sustain larger groups; and this mechanism explains institutions’ historical spread. Even a convincing experiment reminding people of God directly tests only the first, within a bounded situation. Moving to long-run history requires independent evidence about the timing of beliefs, rules and alternative mechanisms.

Causal direction does not follow from large societies and moralising gods occurring together. More complex societies might create demand for particular religious forms. Historical data must distinguish absence of a characteristic from absence of surviving information about it. Otherwise an unknown past becomes an artificially precise sequence.

## Programme relevance

ORI should compare this framework with intergroup-trust research and Atran’s cognitive account. Cooperation with a fellow believer differs from impartial assistance to everyone affected. Further reading should examine historical case-selection criteria and the connection between each mechanism and an observable outcome. Explaining religion’s diffusion scientifically does not settle the truth of faith or determine an individual believer’s or nonbeliever’s moral character. The useful contribution is a set of separable hypotheses, whose evidential support should be assessed individually rather than inherited from the book’s overarching narrative.
