## Content and form of argument

The opening stresses the unusual psychological characteristics of Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic populations. Henrich proposes a historical connection between changes in marriage and kinship influenced by Western Christianity, individualisation and impersonal exchange. This is a comparative historical synthesis; the inspected excerpt introduces the hypothesis without allowing independent verification of its entire evidential basis.

## ORI critical analysis

A valuable question is whether convenient university samples can represent humanity. Recognising diversity, however, should not turn societal categories into fixed characteristics of every person. Generations, classes, religious practices and family relationships can vary considerably within a country. Researchers must preserve analytical levels rather than project average sample differences onto individuals.

The extended historical chain requires separate tests of marriage rules, actual family relationships, psychological differences and institutional outcomes. Supporting one link does not establish the others. Migration, cities, education, state institutions and feedback between them require consideration. Formal religious prescriptions must especially be distinguished from actual compliance.

## Programme relevance

For ORI, the book asks how religious rules change social networks and the circle of people receiving trust. This mechanism differs from briefly reminding someone of supernatural observation. Comparisons should preserve differences in historical periods, measurements and units of observation. Further reading should target a particular part of the argument and its primary data, including cases that do not fit expectations. Neither the title nor an explanation of cultural differences warrants ranking groups’ human worth or intellectual abilities. The research task is to evaluate mechanisms and scope, not convert aggregate cultural categories into personal stereotypes.
