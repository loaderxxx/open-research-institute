## Content and form of argument

The publisher abstract explains religion’s origins through cognitive evolution. Religion is presented as a by-product of human evolution interacting with cultural selection and historical persistence. This differs from an account relying exclusively on cooperation benefits. It is a theoretical synthesis of anthropology and cognitive research, not a single experiment identifying the cause of belief.

## ORI critical analysis

The emergence of a representation, its transmission between people and the durability of an organisation must be separated. A mechanism making an idea memorable does not necessarily explain a lasting community. Conversely, a community’s social benefits do not establish that the underlying cognitive capacity evolved for that purpose. Such explanations can operate at different levels without being mutually exclusive.

Testing a cognitive hypothesis requires specific predictions about memory, interpretation and learning. Religious phenomena cannot automatically confirm it merely because they involve thought. Cross-cultural transfer requires checking whether participants understand the proposed categories similarly and which practices remain outside the study. Religion itself needs specification as a category of observed phenomena.

## Programme relevance

ORI can use the book to challenge a single functional narrative: identify the representation or ritual, ask about transmission conditions, and then examine consequences for interaction. Cooperation, conflict and individual experience should be measured separately. Further reading should investigate primary support for a selected mechanism and its strongest objections. A belief’s cognitive origin establishes neither philosophical falsity nor truth; psychological explanation and evaluation of reasons for faith remain distinct tasks. This distinction preserves the scientific question without turning an explanatory account into a verdict on religious commitment.
