## Question, method and result

The article uses global data on social preferences and reported religious belief. Its abstract associates belief with altruism, trust and positive reciprocity, with differences across religions and institutional contexts. The institutional record identifies the Global Preferences Survey and Gallup World Poll 2012. Experimental validation of measures does not mean participants were experimentally assigned a religion.

## ORI critical analysis

Representativeness and causal identification solve different problems. Broad sampling improves description of an association’s distribution without excluding family, education, income and local norms as influences on both variables. Controlling observed differences does not guarantee the absence of unobserved selection. Prediction should therefore be interpreted statistically unless the design supplies stronger grounds.

Cross-country differences in associations may fit substitution between religious and secular mechanisms. An interaction between two measures, however, does not demonstrate that substitution process. Countries vary on many characteristics simultaneously, and religious questions may mean different things across cultures. Measurement comparability and analytical level require checking.

## Programme relevance

For ORI, the descriptive comparison’s scale is useful. It should be compared with local experiments while distinguishing durable membership from a brief intervention. Trust, altruism and willingness to punish cannot be treated as one cooperation characteristic without an additional argument. Further reading should examine question wording, country coverage, sample weights and sensitivity to alternative definitions of religiosity. The data can clarify a hypothesis’s context without attributing moral characteristics to every member of a religious or nonreligious group. Such distinctions prevent a global association from becoming a claim about individuals or a universal causal mechanism.
