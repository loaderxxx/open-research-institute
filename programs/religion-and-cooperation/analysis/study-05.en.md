## Question, method and result

The preregistered experiments involve iTaukei Christians and Indo-Fijian Hindus. Conflict reminders vary between participants, while reflection on God’s preferences varies within participants allocating money to an outgroup member. The abstract reports that conflict reminders reduced transfers, while the religious perspective increased them in both conditions. This tests a possible boundary of an effect within a particular game.

## ORI critical analysis

The design asks whether the effect disappears when conflict becomes salient. A statistically undetected difference between effects does not necessarily establish exact equality. Full assessment should examine uncertainty intervals and power for the interaction, not merely separate significant results. Within-person comparisons also require checking task order and carryover from earlier responses.

A conflict reminder does not reproduce the threats, losses and institutional conditions of real confrontation. Religious and ethnic membership also overlap in this setting, limiting transfer to places where those relationships differ. Intergroup boundaries should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Programme relevance

ORI can compare this work with trust research, where expectations of another person’s response matter. A money-allocation game creates a different incentive structure and does not test the full reciprocity cycle. Further reading should reconstruct instructions, condition order, comprehension checks and the local sampling rationale. Increased transfers do not establish the disappearance of prejudice or political conflict. The contribution is clarification of a mechanism and possible limits, rather than a general verdict on religion’s peaceful or conflictual nature. These boundaries should accompany any use of the findings in a broader account of cooperation.
