# Research map: religion and cooperation

[Русская версия](RESEARCH_MAP.md) · [Programme README](README.en.md)

**Checked 24 October 2026.** This is a starting map, not a systematic review. Religiosity is not one variable: sources measure belief, practice, identity, ritual or community in different ways. Behavioural experiments do not settle the philosophical truth of religion or establish that religion generally increases or decreases cooperation.

## Books

- Ara Norenzayan, [*Big Gods: How Religion Transformed Cooperation and Conflict*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691169743/big-gods), 2013, Princeton UP. An evolutionary hypothesis about morally monitoring gods and cooperation among strangers; useful as a testable theory, though its causal direction and historical scope are debated. Publisher record is public; book is paid or library access.
- Joseph Henrich, [*The WEIRDest People in the World*](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374710453/theweirdestpeopleintheworld/), 2020, Farrar, Straus and Giroux. A historical hypothesis about the Western Church's influence on kinship, psychology and institutions; it links religious history and cooperation but is not a single causal test. Official Macmillan record is public; ebook is paid.
- Scott Atran, [*In Gods We Trust: The Evolutionary Landscape of Religion*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/in-gods-we-trust-9780195178037), 2002, Oxford UP. An anthropological and cognitive account of religious beliefs and rituals; a useful alternative framework to “Big Gods” theory. Publisher record is public; book is paid or library access.

## Research and public materials

- Goldner et al., [“The Intergroup and Contextual Determinants of Real-World Religious Donations”](https://doi.org/10.1111/jssr.12961), 2025, *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion*. A Jerusalem door-to-door fundraising field experiment found that religious participants donated more often but favoured religious fundraisers. Context: Jewish religious/secular groups and one religiously neutral charity; findings should not be generalised to other religions or countries. Full article is open under CC BY; experiment took place in 2019, publication was in 2025.
- Pasek, Vishkin, Lehner et al., [“In Their God We Trust: Religious Cognition Increases Cooperation Across Religious Divides”](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000499), published online 17 July 2025; issue date August 2026, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 131(2), 288–307. Three preregistered studies (N=1,784; US, Israel, Fiji) with Christians, Muslims, Jews and Hindus found that making beliefs about God salient increased trust and reciprocity within and between groups in monetary trust games. This is an experimental reminder effect in games, not an estimate of religion in everyday life or the causes of peace. Abstract is public; author preprint is available. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40674036/).
- Kelly, Kramer & Shariff, [“Religiosity Predicts Prosociality, Especially When Measured by Self-Report”](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000413), 2024, *Psychological Bulletin*. A meta-analysis of nearly 60 years of research found a stronger association with self-reported than directly observed prosociality. Limit: pooling heterogeneous measures and correlational designs does not establish causation. Abstract is public; full text is publicly available under the publisher's licence.
- Valencia Caicedo, Dohmen & Pondorfer, [“Religion and Cooperation across the Globe”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.09.025), 2023, *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 215, 479–489. Analysis of experimentally validated and globally representative data finds an association between reported religious belief and positive reciprocity, altruism and trust, with differences across religions and formal institutions. These are correlational results; they do not show that religion causes prosociality. Article is open under CC BY-NC. [Maastricht University publication record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/religion-and-cooperation-across-the-globe/).
- Pasek et al., [“Thinking about God encourages intergroup prosociality even when conflict is salient”](https://doi.org/10.1177/13684302241307011), online 25 January 2025; issue 29(5), 2026, *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations*. Among iTaukei Christians (N=324) and Indo-Fijian Hindus (N=280), making thoughts of God salient increased intergroup prosociality even when conflict was made salient. Limits: specific local history and behavioural tasks; publisher record is public, full text may require a subscription.

## Historical landmarks

- **1904–1905 — Max Weber and “The Protestant Ethic.”** A historical-sociological thesis about calling and the capitalist spirit became an influential, contested hypothesis about religious ethics and economic behaviour ([Cambridge UP discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/weber-passion-and-profits/B3BB2F182C995211DA31A053349A11A4)). Weber did not present the essays as a complete explanation of capitalism's origins.
- **2008 — cross-cultural tests of the “Big Gods” hypothesis.** Norenzayan and Shariff's review of religion and prosociality set out an experimental research programme; later work disputes its group boundaries, mechanisms and replicability ([Science article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1158757)).
- **2010s — shift toward behavioural measures and varied contexts.** Cross-cultural field and laboratory studies distinguish self-reported norms from actual transfers, trust and donations. This widened the evidence base without resolving external validity ([Kelly et al. 2024 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000413)).

## Current directions and next reading

In 2024–2026, research has moved toward preregistered cross-cultural experiments, direct behavioural measures and tests of whether norms extend beyond religious groups. The 2025/26 findings on religious cognition and trust sit alongside meta-analytic evidence of stronger self-report associations and field evidence of ingroup preference. These findings cannot be reduced to one effect: interventions, samples and outcomes differ. Open questions concern persistence, mechanisms, political conflict and what each study means by “religiosity.”

**Next reading:** compare Goldner et al.'s design with Pasek et al., then use Kelly et al. to map the gap between self-reports and behaviour. Record whether each study measures religious identity, religious thought or community participation.
