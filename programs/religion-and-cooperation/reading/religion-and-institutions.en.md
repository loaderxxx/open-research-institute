## 1. History as a question of sequence

A historical question differs from a psychological one. A person today may associate faith with generosity, but that does not tell us whether the relevant belief appeared before a large settlement, after it, or alongside markets and writing. We need dates, definitions, and careful treatment of missing evidence.

Studies of social institutions compare kinship, marriage rules, cities, trade, states, and religious organizations. Each variable has its own history. Long-term changes cannot be reduced to a single initial impulse on the basis of correlations among modern countries alone.

Publications on Seshat demonstrate especially well the cost of getting sequence wrong: “the earliest recorded mention” need not coincide with the first spread of an idea. Causal priority can be claimed only where the measurements permit that level of precision.

Here, “institution” includes rules and organizations that outlast a single encounter. It does not refer only to a state bureaucracy: family prohibitions, community funds, and trade agreements also shape expectations. But they should be distinguished by their degree of coercion, participation, and scale. The historical relationship between them and religion may run in both directions, so their coexistence today does not determine their order of origin. Different directions of causality make narrow comparative questions especially important, rather than a single claim that “religion created society.” Distinguishing these units reduces the risk of treating the proximity of institutions as proof of the historical order in which they arose.

## 2. What counts as complexity

Population size, hierarchy, writing, roads, taxation, and specialization describe different aspects of society. A composite index is convenient for comparing many historical regions, but it hides the different sequences in which these changes occurred. A city may have writing without the same form of religious governance.

Studies based on Seshat construct time series for historical polities and geographic zones. Coding turns heterogeneous documents into comparable variables. This work makes it possible to test models, but its precision is limited by the density of surviving sources.

If the result changes depending on whether incomplete rows are included or how intermediate periods are interpolated, this is a property of the data and model, not a minor technical detail. A historical explanation should show these dependencies before proposing a general sequence for the whole world.

The scale of an index also complicates substantive comparison. Two polities may receive similar overall scores through different combinations of population, infrastructure, and administration. If the question concerns the provision of trust, some components may be closer to the mechanism than others. A reliable analysis shows whether its conclusion holds when the index composition changes and when the original indicators are compared without fully compressing them. Even a precise index does not eliminate the difference between a normative text about authority and the actual enforcement of rules in a particular settlement.

## 3. What a moralizing deity means

This category brings together a belief in a supernatural being that cares about human relationships and can evaluate behavior. But a god may know about some violations and not care about others. In different societies, concern for order is also expressed through ancestors, local spirits, cosmic justice, or ritual obligations.

Cross-cultural coding has to set explicit criteria; otherwise, comparison is impossible. Direct interviews by Purzycki’s consortium allow knowledge, punishment, and moral concern to be asked about separately. Historical written material rarely provides answers that are similarly symmetrical.

The category is a research tool, not a rating of a tradition’s “development.” An error arises if the absence of a particular kind of deity is taken as an absence of moral norms, or if a later source is treated as the date when a belief was born.

Different supernatural beliefs cannot be placed on a ladder from “simple” to “complex” merely according to whether they involve external punishment. One tradition may emphasize ancestors and reciprocal relationships, another a formal rule and court, and a third compassion without an equivalent image of oversight. A research variable is needed to make a specific prediction, not to rank the completeness of cultures. Modern surveys demonstrate the diversity of substantive answers and caution against retrospectively imposing one doctrinal scheme on antiquity.

## 4. Coding absence

In a historical dataset, “unknown” means that the available material does not allow us to establish confidently whether a feature was present or absent. Zero means a conclusion that it was absent. These values behave differently in statistical analysis: missing values are excluded, modeled, or replaced, while zero is treated as an observation.

Beheim and colleagues’ critique focused on the fact that the treatment of missing codes for gods in Seshat determined the conclusion about their sequence relative to social complexity. In the retraction notice, the authors of the original study acknowledged that early values had been labeled problematically and that the original and revised analyses differed in how missing values were converted to zeros.

The retraction itself concerns the specific 2019 Nature article. It does not automatically confirm the reverse developmental sequence in all societies. The accurate statement of status is that the original publication was retracted in 2021 and later arguments should be considered separately.

There is also a third option besides zero and an empty cell: an approximate date with an explicit confidence level and rationale. This preserves a trace of weak evidence without pretending it is precise. Models should then show what happens under different reasonable date ranges. Publishing only one convenient sequence hides important uncertainty, especially for poorly documented early eras. The decision behind a code should include its rationale and the date of the source so the reader can reconstruct where the numeric entry came from.

## 5. The 2019 retraction

Whitehouse and colleagues’ article “Complex societies precede moralizing gods throughout world history,” published in Nature in 2019, was formally retracted in 2021. Its headline and original result therefore cannot serve as current confirmation of a historical sequence. This is an important change in the source’s status.

In the notice, the authors acknowledged that some early values should have been marked as absent or presumed absent, rather than unknown and subsequently converted from missing values to zeros in the analysis. They wrote that later revisions support their general conclusion, but that substantial analytical differences required the retraction.

According to the notice, three co-authors did not agree with the retraction. That disagreement should be recorded accurately, but it does not change the article’s publication status. Later versions should be assessed using their own data and published methods, without restoring the force of the retracted version.

A retraction is a decision about the reliability of a specific published analysis, not a collective verdict on everyone in the debate. The Nature notice itself records the authors’ position and the journal’s status determination. Evaluating subsequent versions requires reading their texts, data, and critiques. The phrase “retracted in 2021” should therefore accompany every mention of the original result. For the scientific record, it matters that the team described the corrections as substantial, not as an inconsequential editorial replacement of a number. When referring to the old article, its retraction date should appear alongside its title, even when discussing the history of the debate.

## 6. The objection about missing data

Beheim and colleagues pointed out that a large share of the codes for the historical presence of moralizing gods was unknown, and that alternative treatment of missing data changed the conclusion. Their objection concerns the logic of the analysis, not a claim that ancient people lacked morality.

The difference between “not attested” and “did not exist” is especially acute before written sources appeared. Dropping unknown observations leaves a biased sample of well-documented eras; filling them with zeros creates the opposite risk of artificially late dating for the emergence of a belief.

Readers should ask how a code was obtained, how the first evidence was dated, and whether the direction of the association remains under other reasonable choices. Answering these questions requires checking each subsequent publication independently. No critical letter by itself provides a definitive global chronology.

The critique does not require treating all missing values as absence or all absences as missing. Its force is in showing that the result is sensitive to procedure. After such a criticism, analytical responsibility means defining types of uncertainty, publishing a table of decisions, and comparing alternatives. Even if a new analysis proved robust, that would support the new model, not restore the original publication. Other reasonable ways of treating the archive’s early silence should be compared openly, even when that complicates a narrative about a worldwide sequence.

## 7. Later Seshat analyses

After the retraction, the Seshat team reported revising the data and methodology. A separate article by Turchin and colleagues in Science Advances considers evolutionary factors in social complexity using updated modeling. It should not be conflated with the retracted paper, even if the researchers address similar historical questions.

The new publication has its own outcomes and statistical assumptions. When a conclusion depends on how a moralizing feature is defined, on geographic units, or on time interpolation, readers may compare it with the critique of missing data and with other macrohistorical explanations.

The general principle of source criticism matters here: an update does not make an incorrect original procedure retrospectively correct. But it also does not mean that those involved in the debate have stopped learning from the database. The version, date, and limits of the conclusion must be kept in view.

A single database allows several research tasks: testing early sequence and assessing joint changes in complexity, warfare, agriculture, and ritual. The distinction between tasks is especially important after a retraction. When a new article discusses broader dynamics, its conclusion should not be cited as literal confirmation of every claim in the old one. References in a manuscript should therefore be selected by version and by the specific claim. It is also important to check precisely which outcome a later model predicts: polity structure, the presence of a belief, or a change in the relationship between them.

## 8. Observation versus time of origin

An early text may name a god in a particular language and political center. It attests that a record existed by that time; how widespread the belief was among neighbors is a separate question. A later document does not disprove an earlier oral practice whose traces did not survive.

These asymmetries are especially important in historical databases when comparing societies with and without writing. The technology of record keeping is itself correlated with complexity. As a result, the date of the earliest available evidence may mechanically shift later, even if the belief existed earlier.

A chronological conclusion should therefore be checked for dependence on source density, document type, and the allowable interval of uncertainty. The lack of a confident date is not evidence for either of the competing causal histories.

Records often emerge where power and administrative means of preserving them already exist. This unequal production of sources can simultaneously increase observed complexity and the likelihood of detecting a religious norm. A simple comparison of earliest dates then partly compares archival availability. Historical research can reduce the problem with different kinds of evidence, but it cannot fully recover lost oral history. Special caution is needed when moving from the earliest surviving text to the claim that no one held a similar belief before that time.

## 9. Kinship ties and church norms

Schulz and colleagues analyzed the relationship between historical exposure to the Western Church and its marriage prohibitions, the intensity of kinship networks, and modern psychological measures. Their theory proposes that changes in family ties may have facilitated relationships with non-kin and the emergence of other organizations.

This is an argument about a specific institutional history, not about one religion making people more moral than another. The data combine historical indicators, kinship, and present-day responses; different waves of change, migration, and economic development complicate causal interpretation.

The study matters because it proposes a mechanism through family and partnership rules, distinct from fear of punishment by a god. But comparisons across countries and regions do not describe every person’s life and do not replace separate analysis of other religious institutions.

Exposure to an institution on a map does not mean that every resident followed its rules in the same way. Local marriage practices, circumvention of prohibitions, and other authorities could soften or change their influence. At the same time, a formalized historical hypothesis makes it possible to compare places with different trajectories. Conclusions about associations should be kept at the level of regions and observed measures, avoiding a psychological portrait of an entire faith. This limitation does not make the theory uninteresting: it offers a way to test a historically situated pathway by which partnerships changed, rather than an abstract national character. Historical exposure may also coexist with a different religious practice within the same region and period.

## 10. Markets and the scale of exchange

Trade with strangers and the size of settlements create an independent demand for predictable rules. In comparative game studies, Henrich and colleagues examined fairness, punishment, market integration, community size, and participation in a world religion. Different measures did not produce a fully consistent picture.

If markets and religious organizations expand at the same time, a positive correlation between either one and generosity does not prove which produced the other. Courts, monetary exchange, and public reputations may reinforce cooperation, sometimes drawing on shared social networks.

The finding poses a more precise historical question: which rules make it possible to enter relationships safely beyond kinship, and under what conditions do different institutions take on that task? Answers will differ by time and place.

Games provide a limited but important result: people really do make choices under stakes and rules, rather than only expressing opinions in a questionnaire. Nonetheless, historical commerce involves contracts, repetition, and avenues for complaint that are absent from a short task. Connecting a game measure with institutional evolution therefore requires additional intermediate evidence. Moreover, the same game may have a different social meaning if a coin is rare in one place and part of everyday life in another.

## 11. Well-being and moral teachings

Baumard and co-authors proposed an explanation of some ascetic and moralizing traditions in terms of historical increases in prosperity in particular regions during the Axial Age. In their model, changes in resources are associated with the possibility of alternative life strategies and values. This is a competing macrohistorical hypothesis.

Correspondence among large time series does not distinguish all the pathways: urbanization, literacy, elites, and exchange accompany economic growth. The teachings under consideration also differ from one another, and their emergence cannot be reduced to one date or an identical set of rules.

The work is useful for testing causal direction. If economic conditions can precede some doctrinal changes, faith need not be the initial cause of every change in the scale of cooperation. This does not deny that it may have later effects.

Prosperity itself has different measures: output per person, elites’ access to a surplus, crop security, and resource distribution are not the same. Moralizing teachings may also combine asceticism, care, and prescriptions for justice in different ways. The macrohistorical model identifies an interesting broad relationship, but does not remove the need to distinguish these components by place and era. The existence of an economic surplus should be distinguished from access to it for those who created and transmitted religious teachings.

## 12. Monitoring and institutional complementarity

A belief in divine monitoring may reinforce a rule of honesty, while a public court can provide a process for settling disputes. These mechanisms are theoretically compatible. A market expands the pool of possible partners, while a community gives them relationships and reputations; no institution exists independently of the others.

Purzycki and colleagues’ game study shows an association between some beliefs and allocating resources to distant co-religionists. It does not prove that those beliefs founded a state. Schulz’s work on early church kinship norms concerns another pathway in the formation of partner networks.

If a comparison does not distinguish personal beliefs, organizations, and secular rules, the phrase “the effect of religion” is too broad. A historical explanation improves when it specifies which institution acted, on whom, and during what period.

Institutional complementarity may be asymmetric. A court guarantees rules even to people who do not share a tradition; a community, by contrast, may provide denser support to its participants. Economic exchange connects strangers when promises can be enforced. If an outcome measures only ties within a community, the court’s contribution remains invisible; if it measures transactions, care for a sick neighbor disappears from view. The outcome selected determines which story we see. Institutional complementarity remains a hypothesis about specific actions and rules, not a conclusion drawn from a list of organizations that existed at the same time.

## 13. Secular mechanisms of trust

Contracts, repeat transactions, reputation, and mutual guarantees can support reliability outside religious communities. This is an empirical alternative to the idea that large-scale cooperation depends only on a punitive supernatural observer. It does not rule out religious motives among particular partners.

Ge and colleagues’ study emphasizes the role of reputation in the behavior they examined, while Henrich’s cross-cultural work finds a relationship between a number of outcomes, markets, and settlement size. Agreement in the direction of these alternatives does not prove a single universal model.

An institution should be assessed by which risk it reduces and for whom: theft, a broken promise, lack of support, or exclusion from a resource. Different risks require different evidence and cannot be exhausted by a single scale of generosity.

Reputation does not always travel long distances: reports of past behavior have to be transmitted reliably, and different audiences may understand them differently. Formal documents and intermediaries solve some of these problems. Religious affiliation sometimes acts as such a sign, but does not guarantee an individual’s honesty. The historical question is how signs and checks worked together, not which one worked alone. The reliability of an affiliation signal depends on practices of verification and consequences for deception, which may differ between commerce and everyday mutual aid. Reputation also has its own history of transmission channels: written references, personal recommendations, and debt obligations.

## 14. Organization and access

A community may hold resources, distribute help, arrange joint work, and define membership. These organizational choices often affect support more directly than the abstract intensity of belief. But an organization’s longevity in an archive does not show how fairly it gave people access to help.

Sosis and Bressler studied communes as groups that persist over time, while Power describes a support network in living villages. Together they show two levels of institutions: rules that allow a form to endure, and concrete relationships through which help flows.

Different units of assessment are needed: durability, who receives help, participation, conditions for leaving, and relationships with external neighbors. Organizational success should not be translated into a general judgment about the moral superiority of its members.

An organization has a membership boundary, budget, and decision procedures. One arrangement may preserve a fund well but respond poorly to the needs of someone outside its list. Research on duration and research on targeted aid should therefore sit alongside each other without substituting for one another. Concern about access can be expressed as a precise empirical question: who actually received support and who made the decision? This formulation makes it possible to compare organizational effectiveness and the accessibility of its help while keeping the two assessments distinct.

## 15. Directions of causal influence

A large community may create demand for rules governing strangers, and shared rules may then make its expansion easier. Wealth may change religious teachings, while a new practice may support mutual aid within an organization that has already arisen. Such feedback loops cannot be arranged in one universal linear story.

Three lines of research identify different links: Purzycki and colleagues examine contemporary beliefs and game choices, Baumard and colleagues examine prosperity and historical doctrines, and Schulz and colleagues examine church family norms and social psychology. Their results are not direct replications of one another.

Historical statistics can rule out some simple versions of a theory when temporal order is reliable. When sequence depends on missing data, it is more honest to preserve competing scenarios and state the degree of uncertainty explicitly.

The possibility of feedback makes simple accounts of origins especially vulnerable. Even if a particular norm appeared after a city grew, it could then help sustain further growth. And if ritual is older than an administration, that does not prove that it gave rise to every administrative form. A historical model should distinguish the initial emergence, later reinforcement, and persistence of an institution. An account of feedback especially needs dated transitions, because institutions’ coexistence today does not determine the order of events.

## 16. Scale and independence of regions

Polities in one historical region inherit languages, institutions, and sources from one another. They cannot be treated as entirely independent observations. Conversely, a comparison across regions may collect cases so different that the same code conceals different practices.

Seshat’s long time series and comparisons of the Axial Age provide rare opportunities to formulate large questions quantitatively. Their interpretation depends on accounting for shared origins, geographic proximity, external exchange, and unequal documentation.

A conclusion that holds for a particular set of regions and an index remains precisely that conclusion. Generalizing to “all of world history” requires checks beyond the original sample and convincing historical descriptions of each transition.

Statistical adjustment for geography helps, but inheritance and contact are rarely known in full. A borrowed religious idea and a borrowed legal practice may arrive together along a trade route. Their coincidence would then reflect diffusion, not independent invention in every region. A qualitative history of exchange remains necessary alongside a quantitative map of recurrence. Time series often contain few independent lines of development, even if the number of rows appears large after coding. This dependence reduces the effective number of independent comparisons and broadens a reasonable uncertainty interval around an estimate.

## 17. Checking against different sources

Archaeological traces, inscriptions, legal texts, and oral traditions survive with different probabilities. When a statistical table combines them into a single date, researchers should explain how they moved from evidence to a number. Disagreement among specialists over the interpretation of a source is part of the uncertainty, not a nuisance to hide.

Direct modern interviews about religious beliefs help clarify what questions are worth asking of historical material at all. But the full set of responses from a modern participant cannot be retrospectively assigned to an ancient text.

The debate over missing Seshat codes demonstrated that data availability and code transparency allow critics to check the consequences of analytical choices. Open revision is useful precisely when it preserves clear distinctions between versions and publication statuses.

Disagreement over a date does not always mean that one of the coders made a mistake. A text may contain an ambiguous name, be a late copy, or preserve traces of several layers of editing. It is more useful to preserve the rationale for each interpretation and show the model’s sensitivity than to conceal disagreement in a single zero. This transparency makes historical comparison slower but substantially improves the precision of its language. Preserving the rationale behind a code helps later review and prevents the false impression that a disagreement can be resolved simply by increasing the size of the database.

## 18. Three levels of conclusion

The first level is an observation: a participant allocated resources in a certain way in a particular game. The second is a mechanism-based explanation: norms, fear, reputation, and relationships may change behavior. The third is a historical claim about how an institutional form arose. Evidence at the first level does not automatically transfer to the third.

For example, the association between beliefs about gods and allocating resources to distant co-religionists in Purzycki and colleagues’ study supports a limited contemporary prediction. It does not confirm when those beliefs emerged. The Seshat retraction concerns a separate historical argument, not the field game itself.

This separation also prevents the converse error: criticism of a chronological analysis does not prove that beliefs never affect people’s decisions. Sources can be meaningful while also being insufficient for a broader claim.

Each level permits its own verb: a study observes an allocation, an experiment estimates the effect of a cue, and a historical model compares trajectories. Verbs such as “caused” and “created” require stronger identification. This vocabulary lets us preserve the contributions of the original authors without asking them to prove a world history that their materials were not designed to document. Choosing a precise verb matters more as a conclusion moves farther from directly measured behavior toward the formation of societies over centuries.

## 19. A cautious conclusion from the historical literature

Religious organizations, kinship networks, exchange rules, and institutions for resolving disputes may have interacted as cooperation expanded. Published studies provide specific evidence for these pathways, but differ in how they date and explain global transitions. No single, simple sequence has been established for all societies.

The 2019 Nature article on complex societies preceding moralizing gods was retracted in 2021; its original conclusion cannot be used as a fact. The critique of missing values, later Seshat revisions, and studies of prosperity or kinship should be assessed as separate arguments.

Historical caution does not devalue modern research on cooperation. It clarifies the boundary between what people do in a measured situation and how their institutions arose. Both topics matter when diversity among traditions and uncertainty about dates are preserved.

The literature’s productive result is a set of testable distinctions, not one winning account. Reputation differs from punishment, kinship networks from markets, first evidence from first existence, and community durability from participant well-being. When these distinctions are kept, authors can argue specifically about data and models without turning complex traditions into caricatures. The publications are engaged in a real debate about methods of measurement; a brief synthesis should not declare it closed on the strength of one persuasive headline.

## 20. Open historical questions

Which features of formal justice, exchange, and kinship organization appeared before religious reforms in particular regions? Which traditions transmitted rules of mutual aid without a single punitive god? How was evidence of oral practice preserved where sources emerged late? These are distinct questions in published research.

Comparative games, community archives, and databases of historical polities provide different vantage points. Each requires its own scale and does not allow game behavior to stand in for the date a state arose. The data version, feature definitions, and treatment of missing values should accompany strong claims about timing.

A respectful explanation does not turn a tradition into a tool of economic growth or attribute a lack of morality to a community for which historians lack the relevant document. The status of the retracted work remains explicit even when its authors publish new arguments.

Limits on historical reconstruction do not mean giving up on comparison. They require showing which events are dated, which are reconstructed, and which remain unknown. Preserving this limit is especially important when discussing morality: the absence of written evidence for a moralizing deity is not an absence of care, agreement, or reciprocity among people in the past. Historical respect for people in the past begins by recognizing that written sources have survived unevenly. This principle leaves room for unknown forms of help and responsibility that did not leave documents familiar to researchers.
