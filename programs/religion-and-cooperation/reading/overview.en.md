## 1. Why study cooperation

Cooperation means joint action in which participants bear costs, allocate resources, or follow rules for a shared purpose. In the study of religion, it is not a synonym for virtue: reliable exchange, helping a neighbor, donating to a temple, and treating a stranger fairly measure different things.

Religious traditions include beliefs, public actions, kinship relations, and enduring organizations at the same time. So an association between belonging to a tradition and behavior does not, by itself, show which link is at work. Repeated encounters create familiarity; norms define who receives help; rituals mark participation.

The most reliable way to read the literature is to distinguish behavior in a particular task, a social mechanism, and the historical origins of an institution. Different kinds of evidence test these levels. A field donation is closer to everyday practice than a laboratory coin, but it too does not prove a lasting change in character.

This framing does not require treating cooperation as an exclusively religious achievement or as predominantly secular. It proposes tracing specific intermediate links: whether people meet, trust promises, resolve disputes, and have access to shared resources. Only then does it make sense to compare communities. Social measurement describes the conditions of behavior; it does not judge the truth of a faith or the personal worth of participants. It is especially useful to see that researchers’ different tasks share a question about the predictability of relationships while leaving open how the benefits of that predictability are distributed.

## 2. Five meanings of prosociality

“Prosociality” is used to mean generosity, trust, honesty, punishing a rule-breaker, and contributing to a public good. These actions can diverge: someone may distribute coins fairly and still distrust a new partner. Punishment for breaking a rule can support order, but its cost and target matter.

Field and comparative studies use their own operational definitions. For Purzycki and colleagues, the central measure is the share of coins allocated to a distant co-religionist; for Xygalatas and colleagues, it is a donation during a specific temple event; the Sosis and Bressler commune archives are assessed by duration of existence.

When comparing results, the original outcome measure must be retained. An association between belief and a coin game does not automatically establish the quality of charitable aid, and a community’s longevity does not establish the well-being of each member. The phrase “cooperation in general” hides this decisive distinction.

It is especially important not to confuse motive with measured outcome. Money may be given for a friend, a sacred obligation, neighbors’ recognition, or to help someone in need; the same amount does not reveal the intention. Conversely, someone with no money may show care through labor and time. This review therefore does not let one source stand in for the others: measures are named as the researchers defined them, together with the recipient and the place of observation. Listing several positive findings without preserving their original measures would create an impression of agreement that the measures themselves do not yet establish.

## 3. Near and distant partners

Mutual aid within a family, support for neighbors, solidarity with co-religionists elsewhere, and help for people of another affiliation form different circles of recipients. Broader cooperation does not necessarily mean equal treatment of everyone. A study should specify in advance whose interests are counted and who is left outside the comparison.

Across eight communities, Purzycki and colleagues studied choices among oneself, a local co-religionist, and a distant one. Stronger ratings of the gods’ knowledge and punitive capacity were accompanied by larger allocations to a distant co-religionist. This is a meaningful but narrow result about one boundary of social distance.

Studies of interreligious trust in Mauritius address ethnic and religious boundaries separately. They cannot be replaced by a finding from a game involving co-religionists: intergroup relations also depend on the history of living alongside one another, status, and repeated contact. In-group agreement and openness to outsiders need separate measures.

This distinction changes how the result should be read in practice. Greater generosity toward an unknown co-religionist does not measure attitudes toward a person of another faith; the absence of a measured intergroup effect does not negate real help to neighbors. “Distant” may refer to geographic distance, little familiarity, or institutional mediation. The barriers to and guarantees of cooperation differ in each case, so sources can be meaningfully compared only when the boundary is clearly named. Recipient boundaries are especially visible when symbolic solidarity connects people who have never met in person and rely on intermediaries.

Sources for this section: [R02](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16980); [R06](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10543958/)

## 4. Association and cause

If believers participate in joint work more often, at least three explanations are possible: the practice changes behavior, people with similar dispositions choose the community, or both characteristics are shaped by third factors. Income, markets, education, kinship, and local security can affect all of these variables.

Henrich and colleagues’ comparative study of fifteen populations linked some measures of fairness to participation in a world religion, community size, and market integration; the measures were not uniform. These joint predictors make a simple story about a single cause difficult to sustain.

Randomly assigning a brief reminder of faith can identify the effect of that reminder in an experiment, but not the effect of many years of life in a tradition. Historical data add duration but lose control over many variables. A reasonable conclusion depends on the convergence of these incomplete approaches.

Even good statistical adjustments remove only measured differences. Unobserved family ties or experience with joint projects can leave bias. On the other hand, laboratory randomization often simplifies action to a few minutes and a few units of money. Combining designs increases confidence when results converge on one specific hypothesis; divergence helps define its conditions without treating any single study as a final refutation. Separating methods this way makes it possible to study where explanations overlap without demanding historical or psychological precision that a given tool cannot provide.

## 5. Ritual as joint action

Taking part in communal singing, a procession, or a meal combines a sequence of movements, attention to others, and familiar meanings. Participants may value the action as prayer, remembrance, duty, or celebration. Explaining a ritual’s social function does not exhaust its meaning to them.

In natural settings with large groups, Jackson and co-authors’ study tested synchrony and physiological arousal alongside measures of cohesion and cooperation. A field setting preserves important features of a collective event that a laboratory pair lacks, but makes it difficult to fully separate movement from expectations.

The proposed mechanism should be stated conditionally: a shared rhythm may facilitate coordination and a sense of closeness, while long familiarity or shared teaching provides other routes to support. Ritual is not a single intervention that is the same across all traditions and participants.

It is useful to distinguish the structure of an action from its content. A similar rhythm can be reproduced in sports or a secular celebration, but shared memory and appeal to the sacred have a different meaning. A similar psychological response does not imply the same long-term organization of mutual aid. Research on ritual contributes to the history of cooperation only if it preserves the distinction between momentary coordination and an institution of repeated commitments. As the content of a practice changes, outwardly similar synchrony may accompany entirely different obligations and expectations among participants.

## 6. The observable cost of participation

Demanding practices take time, effort, or money. Signaling theory proposes that such a cost is harder to imitate for someone without a lasting interest in the collective. An observer receives information about a participant’s readiness to follow rules, but the cost is not the same as the moral value of the action itself.

Of the original set of 200 nineteenth-century American communes, Sosis and Bressler were able to code requirements for 83; within this subsample, their number was associated with the longevity of religious communes, but not secular ones. Archival gaps and the observational design do not establish a causal effect of ritual.

A donation, food restriction, and regular attendance each carry different costs and meanings. A signal is reliable only in a particular setting where an audience recognizes the practice and values subsequent behavior. The same mechanism may strengthen internal ties without improving the well-being of outsiders.

At the same time, the costliness of a ritual is not fixed. A long fast may be manageable for one person and medically dangerous for another; a contribution also varies relative to income. An observer may not know the difference and may misjudge commitment. A statistical model of a group needs to specify whose costs it counts and who had no opportunity to enter. Otherwise, the effectiveness of selection is only partly described. Unequal costs of participation also matter when interpreting archives, where information about the personal burden on individual participants is usually not preserved.

## 7. Support networks

Power’s fieldwork in rural South India compared joint participation in rituals with a network of supportive relationships. Among people who participated together, the likelihood of a tie was higher; at the network level, joint participation was associated with denser contacts. This makes it possible to see the concrete helping relationships behind the word “community.”

The reverse explanation remains serious: relatives and friends may attend a ritual together because they already help one another. Local obligations and available time shape both attendance and friendship. The observational design does not establish what share of the relationship formed after the ritual.

Notably, in this study deeper involvement in a network of co-religionists did not automatically mean a break with people of other faiths. This observation is more informative than an abstract scale of “groupishness”: it clarifies that strengthening one circle of relationships need not displace another.

A network result can be read as a map of possibilities: whom a person would turn to when sick, who would learn of their need, and who has the resources to respond. These details matter more than a general declaration of solidarity. But measuring a tie does not guarantee that help was actually given in a crisis. Networks can carry both support and expectations; the same edge in a survey does not tell us its quality. The observation about relationships beyond the religious network is especially valuable: it tests whether an explanation assumes a mandatory isolation that does not exist.

## 8. Belief in watchful gods

The supernatural-monitoring hypothesis holds that knowledge of a god’s moral concern and capacity to punish may change choices, especially when human oversight is weak. It concerns specific beliefs that vary across people and traditions, not a characterization of religion as a single whole.

In a multicenter game, Purzycki and colleagues separately assessed perceptions of gods as knowledgeable, punitive, and morally concerned. The association with allocating coins to distant co-religionists is consistent with a monitoring mechanism, but the same response may reflect community norms or a wish to live up to the image of a good person.

The study measures beliefs and behavior changing together at a given moment. It does not establish that the relevant belief historically arose before large societies, nor does it prove that punishment is necessarily required for trust. Reputation and norms also operate without these beliefs.

Causal inference is especially difficult because beliefs about gods and community norms are learned together. Someone may describe a deity as punitive because people in their surroundings judge actions strictly; or they may act from conviction even in the absence of witnesses. The coin game detects an association but does not observe the inner causal chain. Other mechanisms remain competing explanations, not proven motives of particular participants. Distinguishing belief from norm requires precise questions about what participants think the deity knows and which actions they believe are judged.

## 9. Reputation and audience

A public ritual informs not only the participant but also observers. People who know one another see who attends, who accepts an obligation, and who is willing to participate again. This information can make cooperation more predictable even when the immediate reason for generosity is unrelated to expecting supernatural punishment.

Ge and colleagues’ study compared a reputational explanation with the fear-of-punishment hypothesis in the context of large-scale cooperation. It is a counterexample to a monocausal interpretation: the same visible result allows several mechanisms, and results from one experimental design do not rule out other contexts.

The cost of a signal does not provide an exact reading of character. Someone may participate for family or social reasons; a reliable partner may not participate because of their health or another reason. Assessing the theory requires evidence about how real observers use the signal and how they maintain relationships.

Publicity can also operate through the audience’s own responsibility. Observers promise support to the performer, affirm belonging, and spread information about reliability; this is a reciprocal relationship, not simply an assessment of an individual. In religious communities, reputation is woven into family history and the practice of mutual aid. An economic signaling model isolates one aspect of these relationships, which must be tested before it is applied broadly to history. The question of who learns about a signal and how accurately information travels connects the psychology of observation with the organization of everyday cooperation.

## 10. Experimental reminders

A laboratory prime briefly makes religious identity or concepts salient, after which a researcher observes an economic choice. This design helps establish the effect of that cue under those conditions because the cue can be assigned randomly. It does not model the full depth of ritual and teaching.

Benjamin and colleagues found differences in economic behavior after activating religious identity, with the direction of the result depending on the affiliation and task studied. The debates about priming described by van Elk and colleagues concern reproducibility and the effect of methods used to combine studies.

Heterogeneity does not imply that faith is irrelevant; it calls for a more precise question. Outcomes should be specified in advance, studies should have adequate power, and findings should be replicated under different cultural conditions. An effect of a word cannot be transferred to the historical role of a community or to charitable outcomes.

When discussing reproducibility, one should distinguish a nonsignificant effect from evidence of no effect with adequate precision. A small sample may fail to detect a moderate effect; publication bias can exaggerate what is found. Preregistration, access to materials, and replications with similar and different participants give clearer boundaries. Scientific uncertainty here concerns the design, not respect for the people whose behavior is studied. This also explains why a study with good internal control may be too narrow to support a conclusion about the fate of a large social tradition.

## 11. Nonreligious comparisons

If shared movement and common norms work through attention and a sense of belonging, similar effects may occur at nonreligious gatherings. Such a comparison helps separate a general feature of collective action from the specific content of religious teaching. It does not assume in advance that the effects are equal.

Yudkin and co-authors surveyed participants at multiday secular events in the United States and the United Kingdom. Self-reports of transformative experience and an expanded circle of moral concern increased with time spent there, but on-site generosity remained high without increasing over time. Later relationships were correlational.

The study does not prove that secular festivals replace religious communities. It shows that a sense of unity is not exclusive to them and need not immediately raise an objective measure of giving. Volunteer selection and the event’s context limit generalization.

Secular comparison is especially useful for avoiding a false claim that a mechanism is unique. If a bonding event creates closeness without religious content, a theory must explain what additional role beliefs, organizational rules, and long-term participation play. But self-selection into a festival may differ from life in an inherited community. The same questionnaire does not make these samples interchangeable and cannot establish the meaning of their traditions. An event may affect the circle of empathy while leaving a monetary measure unchanged; that divergence is itself an important empirical result.

Sources for this section: [R08](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-29600-1)

## 12. Communities over time

Archival longevity is especially interesting because cooperation must withstand everyday conflicts, not just a momentary experiment. At the same time, an organization’s survival is not equivalent to a fair distribution of care. Participants may leave, and a strict rule may preserve the organization at the cost of high personal burdens.

The historical sample of American communes studied by Sosis and Bressler provides a comparable, though incomplete, measure of how long they existed. Its observations are compatible with the idea that requirements screen participants or sustain commitments. They do not fully distinguish these processes from initial capital, ownership, and external protection.

The fate of an organization must be distinguished from the fate of its people: who stayed, who was excluded, how decisions were made, and who paid the cost of following the norms. These questions change the ethical assessment of the same longevity statistic and cannot be answered by a single number of years.

Years of existence are convenient for a survival model, but changes within communities are hidden behind the number. A community may change its rules, merge with another, or shrink before it disappears from the record. Archival criteria for closure need to be checked. Assessing well-being requires another body of evidence: personal letters, working conditions, family relationships, and opportunities to take part in decisions. Historical comparison is stronger when it describes communities that are missing from the record and the conditions under which archives preserved evidence of their existence.

Sources for this section: [R01](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1069397103037002003)

## 13. Coordination and trust

Cooperation sometimes begins with simple coordination: agreeing on a meeting time, assigning roles, and recognizing partners through repeated contact. This does not require assuming an increase in altruism. A regular calendar and a shared language of practice can reduce uncertainty even if individual preferences remain unchanged.

Power’s network observations allow for this organizational mechanism: people who participate together have more opportunities to see one another and ask for support. Experiments on synchrony, by contrast, test a brief psychological response. These are different time scales and should be linked cautiously.

If the immediate outcome is contact density and the later outcome is actual help, the requests for and responses to help should be observable between them. Without this chain, the association between ritual and network remains a description of a useful coincidence, not a complete causal account.

An important distinction is between the opportunity and the willingness to help. Even strong trust does not create a supply of food or money; conversely, a wealthy institution can distribute resources through formal rules even when its participants feel little closeness. Network data show access to potential partners, not the volume of resources available to them. This limits conclusions about why aid is greater in one place than another. Discussion of coordination returns attention to the ordinary organization of time: trust often grows through many predictable, small encounters.

## 14. Signal cost and the common resource

A high cost of participation may make a signal easier to distinguish, but it takes away resources that the group could invest directly in a common project. Lang and colleagues’ experiment showed that a more costly signal helped select cooperators and increased subsequent contributions, while the group with a cheaper disclosure option gained more net payoff.

The result comes from an economic game, not a religious community. Its theoretical significance is that the benefit of selection must be counted together with its costs. A demanding practice cannot be called useful solely because those who pass it make more contributions.

A tradition may give an action dignity and meaning that an economic model does not account for. At the same time, the model is a reminder that social function and personal value are different dimensions. Discussing them separately protects both the research and respect for participants.

Analyzing net outcomes need not devalue a practice as a spiritual obligation. The question is narrower: does the proposed social function work better at a higher cost, with other circumstances held constant? This distinction prevents a move from measuring selection efficiency to demanding that a living tradition be made easier or more difficult. Researchers’ conclusions and a community’s choices belong to different registers. Thus, the same practice may be meaningful to participants yet fail a narrow calculation of maximum monetary payoff.

Sources for this section: [R07](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9128853/); [R01](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1069397103037002003)

## 15. Cultural limits of samples

A multicenter study broadens the range of observations, but it does not turn eight communities into a representative sample of all religions. Groups differ in language, livelihood, local authority, familiarity with a monetary game, and ways of describing gods. The same questionnaire words may not refer to the same categories.

The Religion and Morality Project explained the importance of comparable direct interviews rather than relying only on coding old ethnographies. Standardization makes common questions explicit and allows answers to be compared, but interpreting an answer still requires local knowledge.

Generalization should therefore retain its scale: “in the places studied” and “for this task.” A negative result in a new place would inform the boundaries of a model, not indicate a deficiency in a tradition. Respectful comparison starts with an accurate description of contexts.

A comparable translation of a question also does not guarantee a comparable social risk in answering it. Some participants report a belief to a researcher they perceive as an authority; others answer in the presence of people they know. Local researchers and transparent descriptions of interviews help with interpretation. Adding sites is valuable when variation is described meaningfully, not reduced to an abstract average across dissimilar cases. A general label for a tradition does not replace a description of language, local history, and the specific way people were invited to participate in a study.

## 16. Moral norms and institutions

A court, market, professional association, and religious community may support promise-keeping in different ways. They sometimes complement one another: a formal rule simplifies a dispute, while a network of relationships helps people find assistance. But evidence linking religiosity with generosity does not show who created an institution first.

Cross-cultural research on markets, settlement size, and religion finds several joint correlates of behavior in economic games. Schulz and colleagues’ historical study proposes a separate pathway through changes in kinship structures and church marriage norms. These are different scales and different outcomes.

Both lines of work suggest that it is more useful to compare combinations of rules, relationships, and opportunities than to look for a single universal engine of cooperation. An institution can change the pool of partners and the language of obligation at the same time; one correlation cannot reconstruct the sequence of these changes.

An institutional explanation must name a specific task: protecting a transaction, providing care, or coordinating marriage rules. A norm useful for one outcome may not affect another. Historical complementarity is therefore not established by a list of organizations that existed at the same time. It requires dated changes and evidence of an actual link between rules, behavior, and outcomes, and the content of those links may differ across regions. A historical account gains strength when, instead of the general word “influence,” it identifies the changed rule, who enforced it, and who was observed receiving help.

## 17. Historical sequence

The question “what came first?” requires dated evidence, not just participants’ present-day answers. A written text gives the date when a formula was recorded, but the practice may have existed earlier without leaving a document. The absence of a record is therefore different from reliably established absence of a belief.

This difference became central in the debate over the Seshat database. The 2019 Nature paper on the sequence of societal complexity and moralizing gods was retracted in 2021 after criticism of how missing data were handled. Its original conclusion should not be cited as an established fact.

Later publications and corrected datasets form new arguments that should be checked against their own definitions and models. Retraction does not automatically settle the opposite theory, but it changes the status of the original analysis and calls for uncertainty to be shown openly.

Temporal statistics are especially sensitive to missing periods. Even a large international dataset cannot replace a reliable source for the early state of a particular region. If different assumptions about missing evidence change the order of events, a strong historical claim should be narrowed. Retraction demonstrates scientific correction in action; it is not a reason to disregard the difficult work of examining new versions of the data. The status of a retracted source matters even in a popular review, because otherwise a striking headline may be mistaken for a robust result.

## 18. What a donation does not tell us

A donation measures the transfer of a resource to a named recipient, before a particular audience and under particular circumstances. In fieldwork in Mauritius, participants in a more intense ritual and observers gave more to a temple than those at a milder event. This is a significant result about the moment and the purpose of help.

Money was given anonymously in a separate booth, so the study does not measure rewards for a publicly observed gift size. Social expectations, gratitude, and prior reputation could still have influenced the choice; the comparison does not definitively separate these explanations.

An ethical description should not turn a physical ordeal into an abstract “stimulus.” People participate for their own reasons and within their own tradition. Researchers may test social consequences while preserving the distinction between meaning to participants and an observed monetary variable.

A donation also depends on who is collecting and whether people trust the intended use. A strong sense of belonging may increase giving to a familiar institution without changing willingness to give money to an unfamiliar organization. Conversely, a habit of helping outside ritual may not show up at all in a brief field task. All of these possibilities are compatible with sincere participation and limit what can be inferred from a single amount. Different donors may respond to different elements of the same event; the field average combines these trajectories without revealing each one.

## 19. Putting the picture together

There are compelling observations of associations between joint practice, relationship density, behavior in games, and the durability of particular organizations. But these data do not automatically add up to a universal law about religion. Each result has its own recipient, outcome, and time horizon.

Some contexts study closeness after a synchronized action; others study resources for a distant co-religionist or support for neighbors. Nonreligious mass events show partial similarities, while studies of reputation and markets suggest additional mechanisms. None has to exclude the others.

A useful synthesis of the literature compares specific pathways from practice to network, from belief to choice, and from institution to durability. Where the data are only correlational, the causal arrow remains a question. This leaves open both the possibility of benefit and the possibility of uneven consequences.

Similarities across several studies become stronger when their more precise predictions align. If one measures in-group trust, another giving to a distant co-religionist, and a third the lifespan of a group, simply adding the positive findings would be wrong. Their value is in building a more detailed causal map, where some arrows have been studied and others remain proposed and should be named as such. Synthesis does not erase contradictions: in some settings mechanisms may reinforce one another, and in others they may compete for attention and resources.

## 20. Open questions in published research

The literature leaves three related questions. When does brief bonding become lasting support? Across which boundaries—kinship, religion, ethnicity—does trust extend? In what historical sequence did beliefs, kinship relations, markets, and public institutions change?

Studies of networks, games, and archives provide parts of an answer, but they measure different units and almost never follow one causal chain from end to end. The dispute over missing data in Seshat shows especially clearly how a conclusion about timing depends on rules for coding an undocumented past.

This synthesis should be read as a map of published questions, not as a proposal for a new experiment or an assessment of whose faith is better. The social function of a ritual does not exhaust its meaning, and statistical differences between groups do not describe people’s worth.

Each open question could have different answers in different places. The contemporary life of a tradition is not a preserved specimen of its own ancient beginnings, and an archive does not provide full access to the thoughts of the past. The review of published literature therefore ends with a limited set of explanations and a cautious historical framing. It does not prescribe how a religious organization should be arranged or declare an individual’s behavior to be a consequence of their affiliation. None of the cited studies measures human worth, so their limited findings cannot be turned into an assessment of a group.
