## 1. Which result deserves trust?

The book proposes evaluating analysis through predictive checking, computational feasibility and stability, considering decisions along the path from question to conclusion. This connects computational quality with the domain purpose. The [preface](https://vdsbook.com/) and [PCS introduction](https://vdsbook.com/01-veridical_ds) provide the starting framework; the applications to scientific agents developed here are ORI’s own.

For a scientific agent, three claims should be separated: the program ran; it computed what was specified; the computation actually answers the research question. Execution logs can test the first claim, while control examples and implementation inspection help test the second. The third requires understanding measurements and assumptions. An agent may calculate models’ mean accuracy impeccably, then use that number to answer whether they produce reliable literature reviews. The error lies in the transition between the measured quantity and the desired property, not in arithmetic.

Trust should therefore attach to a particular conclusion rather than become a permanent certificate for an entire process. A reproducible finding that “configuration A produced more correct final answers on these one hundred tasks” might be well supported. Saying “A conducts research better” adds assumptions about task representativeness, intermediate actions, cost and intended use. Those assumptions do not become verified merely because the first result repeats. Localising trust allows a real achievement to be recognised without silently enlarging its meaning.

The word “veridical” should not be read as a promise to derive certainty from one procedure. A more productive reading treats it as a demand to maintain the connection between analysis and the reality it is meant to describe. That connection is easily lost in AI-assisted work: one model formulates the question, another cleans the data and a third writes the conclusion. Each transition can look reasonable separately while the complete system ends up studying a different quantity. Verification must follow the subject of the claim even when its technical performers change.

An informative negative result also matters. If available data cannot distinguish two hypotheses, carefully establishing that boundary is a useful research outcome. An evaluator that insists on choosing a winner creates pressure to exaggerate certainty. Improving the agent’s average score can then reduce the quality of the resulting knowledge. The book’s framework helps pose a more precise question: what evidence would need to exist for this particular conclusion to be justified? The answer may require a new measurement rather than more persuasive prose. In that sense, the important achievement is not confidence itself but an inspectable basis for the confidence being expressed.

## 2. Question formulation determines the denominator

The data-life-cycle chapter begins with the domain question and data provenance; planning how to evaluate a finding precedes obtaining it. That order matters because later accuracy depends on the task actually formulated. [Chapter 2](https://vdsbook.com/02-dslc) provides the starting point for the following example.

Suppose agents are asked to reproduce one hundred analytical operations drawn from papers. An agent completes ninety runs, of which seventy-two pass substantive checking. The report could give 80 percent accuracy among completed runs or 72 percent success among all assigned tasks. Both calculations are arithmetically correct. They answer different questions: the quality of completed work and the probability of obtaining a correct result after assigning a task. Excluding the ten unfinished tasks without explanation may lead readers to interpret the first quantity as the second.

The distinction becomes more serious when noncompletion depends on difficulty. Suppose the agent stops more often on papers with complicated tables. Excluding those cases changes the capability being measured. The study now evaluates documents the agent managed to process, although the original question concerned the complete collection. A confidence interval alone cannot repair this: an interval around a misunderstood quantity does not restore the original target. Separate outcome categories and an explicit denominator rule are needed.

ORI can use a concise specification of the research claim: task unit, target population, execution conditions, success criterion and treatment of missing results. This is not administrative decoration; it exposes changes in the question. The unit might be a paper, a question about a paper or an answer attempt. If one difficult paper contributes twenty questions and another contributes two, averaging over questions gives them unequal weight. That choice may be defensible, but its meaning needs explanation.

Question formulation also contains a value judgment. An editor may care about statements with supporting references; a researcher about preservation of important qualifications; a time-constrained user about the effort needed to repair errors. One metric cannot automatically stand for reliability in general. It is better to formulate several testable questions and expose possible conflicts between them. An agent that more often declines unsupported answers may complete fewer tasks while being more useful in a particular research role.

A strength of the book’s approach is returning to the question after examining the data. That return should leave a record: the original task must not quietly become a more convenient one, with the change presented as the initial plan. Preserve the first formulation, explain why it changed and identify the new analysis separately. This permits learning during research without confusing productive clarification with selection of whichever question makes the result most attractive. It also lets a reader decide whether the revised question still addresses the practical need that motivated the study.

## 3. From document to number: where meaning is lost

A program can reproduce every operation while operating on the wrong value. Consider a hypothetical review in which an agent extracts an error rate of 0.05 from one table and combines it with 5 from another paper. The first uses a proportion and the second a percentage. Directly averaging them produces a meaningless result despite technically correct computation. Citing both papers does not remove the error: the measurement unit must remain attached to the number.

More difficult cases share units but differ in definitions. One study counts any incorrect final answer as an error, another counts only answers with incorrect explanations, and a third excludes refusals. A common table heading such as “accuracy” can conceal incompatible events. Before aggregation, operational definitions are needed: how the quantity was obtained, what enters its denominator and which cases were excluded. Sometimes the correct synthesis is to decline a pooled mean and explain why the data cannot be combined.

In its own application of PCS, ORI treats extraction as a measurement procedure. It has an object, an instrument such as a parser or model, coding rules and possible errors. Checking a small random sample of extracted values is useful, but should be supplemented with anticipated difficult cases: layered headings, footnotes, table images, negative numbers and ranges. A control sample made only of convenient passages says little about performance across the whole collection.

Unavailability should be distinguished from absence. If a paper’s complete text was not obtained, an agent cannot infer from a missing detail that the authors failed to report it. Likewise, a missing table result may indicate parsing failure rather than an unperformed measurement. These states need different labels in the resulting dataset. Otherwise a technical access limitation becomes a substantive statement about research, and subsequent statistics reinforce the substitution.

A checkable chain for one number can include source and version, table location, original row, extracted value, unit conversion and the reason for inclusion. There is no need to duplicate the entire document in every row; a reproducible pointer to the evidence and operations is sufficient. The record becomes particularly useful after an error is discovered. Researchers can identify which conclusions depended on it rather than reread the entire corpus. Correction becomes a bounded examination of affected dependencies instead of a general suspicion about everything the system produced.

The limitation is equally important. Exact extraction does not establish the validity of the original experiment. An agent can faithfully transfer a published number produced through biased sampling or faulty measurement. Evidence provenance and evidence quality should therefore remain separate. This is one reason a literature review cannot be evaluated only by the number of correctly formatted citations: formatting enables scrutiny, but it does not perform all the scrutiny on the reader’s behalf. A traceable statement is an invitation to verify its basis, not a substitute for examining that basis.

## 4. Data cleaning as a checkable decision

Chapter 4 proposes preserving raw data and expressing cleaning alternatives through a reusable function with explicit parameters. Alternatives then become available for sensitivity analysis. [Data preparation](https://vdsbook.com/04-data_cleaning) consequently ceases to look like a neutral technical stage; the following applies that idea to agent evaluation.

Suppose execution time is missing for ten tasks. One explanation is that the agent did not finish before the limit; another is that execution occurred but timing failed; a third is that the task was never assigned. Replacing all missing values with a median creates a tidy numerical column while conflating three different events. A cleaning rule needs to follow the origin of the missingness. When that origin is unknown, uncertainty should be retained rather than removed with a convenient number.

It helps to distinguish representation repair from a substantive assumption. Converting “12 seconds” into the number 12 changes format when the unit is known. Replacing an unobserved duration with 12 assumes something about an unseen process. These operations may look similar in code but have different evidential status. Documentation should expose where a mechanical transformation occurred and where a conclusion-sensitive assumption entered. This helps reviewers concentrate on consequential choices.

For a hypothetical comparison between two agents, defensible alternatives might report completed-run duration separately, treat unfinished tasks as reaching the specified limit, or retain success and cost as two quantities without forcing them into one score. The alternatives are not automatically equally reasonable. If logs unequivocally establish a timeout, median replacement is no longer a defensible option. The variation space should reflect knowledge of the data, not a desire to generate many numbers.

The same logic applies to deduplication. Two rows may duplicate one execution or represent independent attempts under identical conditions. Deleting them because their text matches can erase real variation; retaining true duplicates gives one event excessive weight. Identification keys should therefore reflect the experimental design. In literature corpora, a related problem arises with a preprint and journal version: they may be connected documents from one investigation rather than two independent confirmations.

The practical benefit is reproducing not only the final clean table but also the reason for its shape. Changing a rule should reveal which records and conclusions are affected. Parameterisation does not remove domain responsibility, however. A function with twenty switches can generate twenty implausible worlds. The substantive achievement is a small collection of defensible alternatives with explained consequences, not the maximum number of preprocessing variants. This matters particularly for AI because code generation is cheap while checking the meaning of each variant remains costly. A reversible operation is useful only when the reason for reversing or retaining it can also be examined.

## 5. Stability should be assessed at the level of the conclusion

Imagine two agents. On easy tasks, A solves 90 percent and B 80 percent; on difficult tasks, A solves 30 percent and B 70 percent. With ninety easy and ten difficult tasks, their expected averages are 84 and 79 percent. With equal numbers of easy and difficult tasks, the averages become 60 and 75 percent. These numbers are hypothetical, but their arithmetic shows why “A is better than B” depends on the intended task mixture, not only the measured accuracy within each group.

Changing random seeds within the original collection may leave the ranking almost unchanged. That demonstrates stability to one technical source of variation, not to a domain-relevant change in composition. Before running many repetitions, specify the conclusion whose stability is being assessed. A claim about the average user needs a distribution of that user’s tasks. A claim about difficult research cases cannot justify their low weight merely because they are rare in the current benchmark.

ORI’s critical refinement is that stability always has a domain. It is appropriate to say a result survives listed changes to sampling, cleaning and parameters. Omitting that domain turns the finding into an unsupported claim of general reliability. Selecting perturbations after seeing the result so that they barely change anything is especially problematic. The check then becomes a demonstration of the desired property. More useful practice identifies plausible change mechanisms beforehand and explains why others are excluded.

A result can also be stably wrong. If every parser uses the same incorrect unit-conversion table, different algorithms can produce consistent answers. If all research agents receive one erroneous reference answer, their agreement does not repair it. The remedy lies in data provenance rather than more model diversity above the same foundation. Independence should therefore be evaluated relative to a possible error source, not by counting programs or model names.

The outcome may be a conditional selection rule instead of one stable ranking. A could be preferable for cheap routine tasks and B for difficult cases where repair is costly. That picture is more informative than a forced winner. It connects measurement with purpose and identifies additional data needed for a decision. Absence of a universal advantage is not a research failure when stable boundaries of usefulness have been found.

Reading the book productively also requires separating parameter, prediction and decision stability. Different models can have very different coefficients while making similar forecasts; a small prediction change can reverse an action near a threshold. The analytical unit must match the final claim. In AI research, it might be source selection, abstention or the need for manual review rather than a numerical score. At that level, the report should explain what survives changes and why that persistence matters to the user. Otherwise a technically impressive stability calculation may answer a question nobody actually needs resolved.

## 6. Exploratory analysis creates hypotheses

Chapter 5 shows that interpretation depends on filtering and visual presentation as well as data. It also distinguishes external confirmation from publications that agree while reusing the original source. [Section 5.4](https://vdsbook.com/05-data_viz) is useful for critically reading agent reports; the following is an independent illustration.

Suppose an agent examines forty benchmark partitions: by instruction length, document type, discipline, citation count and other features. One partition shows a substantial advantage for a new method. The pattern might be real, but the search procedure has already used the data to select an interesting result. Presenting that comparison as the only prespecified question conceals the dependence. The next appropriate step is to formulate a hypothesis and test it on data not used in the search.

Banning exploration would be a poor solution. In unfamiliar tasks, inspecting errors often reveals a misunderstood instruction or unusual failure class. The requirement is to distinguish data roles, not cultivate artificial blindness. One collection helps develop an explanation; another tests whether it persists. If new data are unavailable, the result can retain an explicitly limited status and motivate a specific future check. That is more useful than implying final confirmation through elaborate language or numerous charts.

Visualisation is a decision too. Different vertical-axis ranges for two agents can amplify a small difference or conceal it. Showing only successful tasks hides failures. Averaging across disciplines may suggest uniform improvement where none exists. A good figure should help test the claim: common scales, clear denominators and visible missingness often matter more than decorative complexity.

ORI can particularly benefit from a hypothesis-provenance log. It records the observation that prompted an explanatory change, alternatives considered and data reserved for checking. The log need not contain every minor chart. Its purpose is to preserve decisions that influenced selection of the reported result. AI can rapidly generate hundreds of analytical variants; without a record, researchers may not even know how widely the system searched for an attractive pattern.

Finally, agreement among several reviews should be checked through provenance. Five articles repeating one original dataset do not supply five independent replications. A literature agent therefore needs links between claims and underlying studies rather than a citation count alone. An exploratory result becomes stronger when it survives a new measurement or a genuinely independent checking method. Repeating one conclusion in different words can improve clarity without increasing empirical evidence. This distinction gives exploration an honest place in research: it generates candidate explanations whose later support can be assessed, rather than pretending that every interesting pattern was already independently confirmed when it was first noticed.

## 7. Evaluation should resemble future use

Chapter 8 distinguishes data used for learning, selection and final assessment; the splitting procedure should correspond to intended use. The [introduction to prediction problems](https://vdsbook.com/08-prediction_intro) prompts a practical question: which future does a scientific-agent test represent? Randomly separating rows is not an answer by itself.

Suppose a corpus contains questions about several versions of the same papers. Randomly distributing questions between training and testing can expose the agent to familiar investigations with slightly different wording. High accuracy may reflect performance within already known documents. If the intended use concerns new papers, related versions and questions from one investigation should remain grouped. If the goal concerns a new discipline, even paper-level separation may be insufficient.

Future literature introduces a temporal dimension. Papers after a chosen date may use new terminology, methods and data formats. That evaluation differs from a random sample of older documents even when task counts match. A recent date does not itself guarantee novelty: a later article may repeat a long-established analysis. The study needs to explain what should transfer—language, tool operation, methodological understanding or discovery of new evidence.

Leakage can also enter through the evaluator. If a model judge has learned reference answers or receives an overly detailed expected solution, agreement with the agent may reflect shared preparation rather than independent checking. Similarly, developers may repeatedly revise instructions after examining test errors. The agent’s weights were not formally trained on those tasks, but the development process used information about them. The learning boundary is therefore wider than one weight-optimisation run.

A practical protocol should identify the unit of independence: document, investigation, laboratory, task family or time period. It should then inspect connections crossing the dataset boundary. This does not mean all dependence can be eliminated. Scientific methods and datasets are naturally connected. The requirement is to describe remaining dependence and limit the conclusion accordingly. A result might support transfer to new questions about a familiar corpus without supporting transfer to new fields.

These checks are diagnostically useful too. A sharp decline under investigation-level splitting but not question-level splitting may indicate dependence on familiar content. A decline limited to new document formats points more plausibly towards extraction problems. Different kinds of future require different improvements. The book encourages treating evaluation as a model of application; ORI’s task is to choose that model according to a concrete system claim rather than declare one split universally correct. Reporting the split’s rationale gives readers the information needed to judge whether the tested capability is the one they expect to rely on.

## 8. Selecting the best solution consumes evaluation data

Imagine instruction development for an agent: twenty variants are created, each evaluated on one collection, and the best is selected. Even without weight changes, the collection contributed to building the final system through instruction selection. Its score is no longer independent evidence about the chosen variant. This is ORI’s application of the selection-versus-assessment distinction running through chapters 8 and 13.

There is no need to assume deliberate deception. Developers naturally fix observed errors and welcome improvement. The problem arises when performance on a familiar collection is described as expected quality on new tasks. Exploring more variants creates more opportunities to choose a fortunate match to the evaluation’s peculiarities. Automated experimentation makes this a practical risk even for small teams: an agent can explore many instructions faster than a person can understand their differences.

A sensible arrangement separates working evaluation from final assessment. The working collection supports comparisons, error inspection and system changes. The final collection is used after configuration and scoring rules are fixed. If its outcome prompts another revision, that can be a legitimate new development cycle, but the old collection is no longer untouched confirmation of the revised system. New evidence or a more limited status description is required. Naming a file “test” once does not preserve its independence forever.

Agent comparisons must also fix the attempt budget. A system selecting the best of twenty outputs solves a different task from one producing a single answer. Reporting quality at several prespecified budgets, together with full selection cost, is useful. If a person chooses the result, their work belongs to the procedure. If a model judge chooses it, its errors belong to the procedure too. Excluding these components makes a system-level result look like a property of one model.

[Chapter 13](https://vdsbook.com/13-final_ml) considers a selected single fit, an ensemble and interval-based output. The useful transfer to scientific agents is not mechanically copying a selection algorithm, but preserving the boundary between data that help choose and data that assess what was chosen. Even a sensible tuning process needs separate evaluation. Transparency makes the source of confidence understandable rather than merely displaying a final score.

A report need not recount every internal attempt. It should disclose what is necessary to assess selection: the number of substantive variants considered, the selection criterion, which data remained independent and how failed runs were counted. This makes the comparison checkable and helps later researchers decide whether to reproduce the chosen configuration or first examine stability of the choice itself. A high score remains valuable when its route of production is sufficiently visible. The purpose of such disclosure is to interpret the result correctly, not to penalise researchers for iterating and learning from failures.

## 9. Ensembles help only when their errors are understood

Agreement among several models can look like a natural safeguard. Its value depends on error structure. Three agents extracting a number from the same misparsed table can all be confidently wrong. Different sources may still repeat one original statement, making independence smaller than it appears. Participant count does not reveal how many independent ways of detecting an error are actually present.

Evaluating an ensemble benefits from a concrete account of complementary failures. One agent may retrieve documents well, another check units carefully and a third detect mismatches between data and conclusions. Combining them can help if the process preserves these differences and repairs specific transitions. Three identical answers at one stage may add little. What matters is not varied model backgrounds but different checking capabilities relative to the anticipated failure.

Consider a hypothetical case in which two agents support a conclusion and one objects that the studied population differs from the population named in the claim. Majority voting discards the substantive objection. A stronger procedure first checks its basis. If the source confirms the objection, it should change the conclusion regardless of vote count. If it does not, the objection can be rejected with a reason. This matters in science because a correct qualification may be rare while an attractive generalisation is easy to reproduce.

Disagreement is not automatically useful either. A model can introduce arbitrary doubts without a checkable basis. The system should distinguish substantive dissent from noise through a specific claim, source or discriminating test. Discussion then becomes evidence inspection instead of a contest between confident formulations. Objection-checking costs also matter: one well-organised control can sometimes contribute more than another agent.

ORI’s application of the book compares an ensemble with a strong individual configuration under matched budgets and data-access rules. It then examines cases in which aggregation damaged a correct answer, not only average performance. Such cases reveal information lost through combination. Always selecting the most confident prose may systematically suppress accurate qualifications. Averaging incompatible numbers can create a new answer that none of the sources supports.

An ensemble is meaningful as a testable procedure rather than a symbol of collective wisdom. Its output should remain connected to the provenance of contributions and the conflict-resolution rule. The book helps frame ensemble selection as one analytical decision among alternatives; a particular architecture for scientific-agent interaction needs its own evaluation. Neither open communication nor participant count replaces independent evidence that the final answer better addresses the original question. The practical standard is whether combining contributions exposes and corrects errors that the simpler alternative would miss, at a cost justified by the improvement.

## 10. An interval does not yet mean calibrated confidence

Section 13.3 examines prediction intervals constructed from acceptable analytical variants and evaluation of their coverage. The important distinction is between variation in forecasts and empirically checked uncertainty. The following is an original example, not a reproduction of the book’s calculations or data.

Suppose several reasonable models predict that a scientific task will take between forty-eight and sixty seconds. That range expresses variation across the selected models and preprocessing choices. It does not yet mean that actual duration will lie inside with 90 percent probability. All models may have been trained on short documents and fail to account for slow loading of large supplements. A narrow range would then reflect a shared blind spot rather than high accuracy.

Coverage requires tasks with observed durations and a prespecified interval-construction procedure. If eighty-two of one hundred independent evaluation observations fall inside their intervals, observed coverage is 82 percent. This describes the sample, not an automatic guarantee for the next task. Sample size, similarity to future use and changing conditions matter. Adjusting interval width on those same observations changes their role: they become calibration material, requiring a separate subsequent assessment.

Average coverage can conceal important differences. Hypothetically, intervals cover 95 percent of easy tasks but only 60 percent of difficult tasks. The overall number depends on their mixture and may look acceptable. If difficult investigations are the main intended use, that calibration provides little reassurance. Evaluation should therefore examine subgroups, interval width and consequences of missing the interval alongside the hit rate. A very wide interval can achieve high coverage while supporting no useful decision.

Textual conclusions introduce another boundary. A numerical distribution of runtime predictions should not be directly transferred to the probability that a sentence such as “the method explains a memory mechanism” is true. Such a statement can contain ambiguous definitions, disputed causal assumptions and several levels of support. A language model’s confidence percentage does not become a calibrated interval merely by looking numerical. The event, checking criterion and collection of comparable cases must first be defined.

ORI’s application therefore separates measurement uncertainty, analytical sensitivity and interpretive uncertainty. They can interact without sharing one scale. Coverage is useful for numerical forecasts; a disputed literature synthesis may instead need supporting and limiting evidence, alternative interpretations and conditions under which the conclusion would change. The book’s strength here is the reminder that precision in presentation must be earned through checking. Confidence cannot be obtained simply by averaging several agreeing answers or choosing the narrow range between them. This distinction allows numerical tools to be used where their meaning is clear without exporting their apparent precision into questions they have not been designed to resolve.

## 11. Computational feasibility and documentation

Exhaustively checking every variant is impossible. Even a modest design grows quickly: four defensible preparation procedures, three methods, five random repetitions and two access modes produce one hundred twenty runs. At ten minutes per run, sequential execution requires twenty hours before reviewing outputs. This hypothetical calculation explains why the verification budget belongs in the research plan. Multiplying variants does not itself improve a conclusion.

Sensible prioritisation begins with error sources capable of changing the decision. If document dependence is the main risk, investigation-level splitting is more useful than adding random seeds on the old collection. If one cleaning rule drives the result, clarifying the affected data’s provenance deserves priority. Computation is then spent distinguishing substantive alternatives. A cheap check can be highly informative when directed at a consequential assumption.

Documentation should connect technical and interpretive reproducibility. The technical part includes versions of code, data, models and tools, parameters and execution conditions. The interpretive part explains why a rule was chosen, what it measures and which alternatives were rejected. A working container cannot explain why an agent’s refusal counted as an error; a detailed narrative cannot reproduce a calculation without the relevant files. These components complement one another and require different scrutiny.

A public hypothetical study can distinguish unchanged inputs, transformations, run configurations, observations and interpretation. Each final claim should identify the observations supporting it. A parser correction then reveals dependent computations that need rerunning. A changed interpretation does not necessarily require executing all the code again. This organisation lowers correction costs and preserves the history of why conclusions changed.

Agent systems add an unstable external environment. Search may return different documents, an API may change version and a model may respond differently to the same instruction. Exact output repetition is therefore not always possible even when local code is preserved. Researchers should define the reproducibility target beforehand: a specific artefact, a performance range, a stable conclusion or repetition of a procedure. Saved logs make a past run inspectable without guaranteeing unchanged future service behaviour.

The book gives computability a supporting role, while the inspected life-cycle and preparation sections emphasise recording decisions. ORI adds explicit accounting for human verification: time spent reading sources, examining disputed cases and repairing results. Token savings or machine-time savings are not equivalent to savings in research labour. A system may generate more slowly yet cost less overall because its conclusions are easier to check and correct. This comparison should include the work transferred to reviewers rather than treating it as invisible. Computational feasibility becomes scientifically useful when it supports enough relevant checking to justify the claim, rather than merely enabling more outputs to be produced.

## 12. What the book offers the programme, and what it leaves unresolved

The main strength of *Veridical Data Science* for reliable AI research is a coherent language for the entire route to a conclusion. Errors can enter through the question, data, cleaning, model, evaluation or presentation. This counters the habit of trying to repair everything by strengthening the generator. Sometimes improvement requires a different task collection, clarified units or a narrower conclusion. A more sophisticated model does not automatically recover a missing measurement.

Predictive success, however, is not causal explanation. An agent could forecast which papers will be cited using journal characteristics while understanding nothing about the causes of scientific value. A stable forecast does not transform a feature into a mechanism. Causal claims need additional assumptions, designs and checks appropriate to intervention questions. PCS remains useful, but does not replace justification of the causal structure or settle the issue through one successful prediction test.

A second boundary concerns domain knowledge. It helps select reasonable alternatives and detect inappropriate measurements, but may itself be incomplete or contested. Invoking an expert should not end scrutiny. Record the basis of an expert decision and cases where another specialist would choose differently. If disagreement changes the conclusion, include it in analysis or explain why one position has stronger support. Domain expertise then supplies inspectable reasons rather than hidden permission for arbitrary choices.

A third boundary is open research. Not every important discovery can be reduced beforehand to a fixed score. New evidence sometimes changes the question itself. This does not argue against verification: it requires distinguishing exploration from confirmation, retaining the origin of the new hypothesis and identifying what remains unknown. An evaluator accepting only a previously known answer may miss a useful reformulation. A scientific agent therefore needs both execution checks and substantive examination of its result.

A practical reading route for AI researchers begins with their own claim and intended application, proceeds through data preparation and exploratory analysis, then examines prediction selection and final assessment. At each stage, ask which decision could have changed the conclusion and what evidence justifies it. The answer need not always produce another experiment; sometimes it requires correcting an overextended statement. But an assumption on which the main conclusion depends cannot remain invisible.

This analysis consequently treats the book as a strong methodological foundation rather than a finished specification for a research agent. Its approach helps expose decisions, compare substantive alternatives and avoid equating a reproducible artefact with justified knowledge. Further ORI work should test particular transfers to literature search, programmatic analysis and model-based evaluation. The practical success criterion is whether it becomes clearer what has been established, on what grounds, and which new evidence could change the conclusion. That criterion recognises the book’s contribution while leaving room to criticise any specific implementation that performs the formal steps without improving the connection between evidence and claim.
