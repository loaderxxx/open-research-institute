## 1. Why another approach to AI safety?

Imagine an AI assistant in a research laboratory. It explains papers, proposes experiments and writes code remarkably well. When should it be trusted not just to advise, but to act? “When it becomes intelligent enough” is an unsatisfactory answer: finding solutions does not establish that a system respects our constraints, reports its errors honestly or cannot bypass safeguards. Several distinct research traditions are now developing around this gap.

LawZero starts with the system’s design: can we build a powerful predictor without training it to pursue consequences it prefers? Scientist AI separates probabilistic understanding from action selection. Contextualisation helps distinguish facts from reports of facts, while training should not reward the predictor for the consequences of using its answer. A publication dated 2 July 2026 develops a conditional safety argument depending, among other things, on training dynamics and the rarity of dangerous predictors. This is a research programme, not empirical proof of a universally safe model. [S01](https://lawzero.org/en/research)

Even a successful predictor would not solve everything. Its world model can be wrong; humans can define harm incorrectly; software can execute the wrong operation. We therefore move from “what does the model want?” to “what is it allowed to do?”, “what exactly has been verified?” and “who checks the safeguards themselves?”. This is not a competition with one winning company. Different teams address different parts of a shared problem, and their results cannot be added into one safety percentage.

## 2. When the model cannot be fully trusted

Redwood Research makes a useful reversal: suppose a powerful model can already break the rules deliberately. Can the surrounding system still contain it? In AI Control, a weaker but trusted model watches the stronger one, edits its work or defers to a more restricted executor. Published environments including BashArena, BashBench and LinuxArena make this idea experimentally testable. Unlike LawZero, this does not first require proving the absence of dangerous intentions. Success in one environment, however, does not automatically transfer to arbitrary tools, longer tasks or a stronger adversary. [S02](https://www.redwoodresearch.org/research/ai-control)

To make such checks repeatable, the UK AI Security Institute and Redwood developed ControlArena. An agent receives a legitimate main task while an adversarial side tries to produce an additional harmful outcome inside an isolated environment. Built on Inspect, the library brings together environment configurations, agent policies, monitors and response protocols. For ORI, that is more valuable than an impressive demonstration: it provides a basis for reproducible experiments. Scenario realism and trust in the weaker model remain assumptions, however, and the library does not issue safety certificates. [S03](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/introducing-controlarena-a-library-for-running-ai-control-experiments) [S04](https://control-arena.aisi.org.uk/) [S05](https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/control-arena)

Anthropic’s SHADE-Arena asks a related question using complex tasks with hidden harmful objectives and a monitor that reads action transcripts. The publication dated 16 June 2025 describes 17 tasks and evaluations of 14 models. It is easy to draw the wrong conclusion: the ability to carry out artificially assigned sabotage does not establish its prevalence among ordinary users. Suspiciousness thresholds and main-task success also affect results. Usefulness, attack success, monitor misses and false alarms therefore need separate reporting. [S08](https://www.anthropic.com/research/shade-arena-sabotage-monitoring)

## 3. Noticing danger is not enough: the response must arrive in time

When an experiment becomes a working service, timing becomes central. Google DeepMind’s roadmap dated 18 June 2026 distinguishes asynchronous responses to reversible actions from synchronous prevention of especially dangerous ones. The authors describe layered defences, access restrictions, an internal prototype and monitoring deployment. The important shift is from “the monitor noticed something” to measuring coverage, detection recall and response time. This is a developer report and roadmap, not a universal mathematical guarantee. [S06](https://deepmind.google/blog/securing-the-future-of-ai-agents/)

OpenAI’s publication dated 29 October 2026 describes a multistage system, from inexpensive classifiers and a model reviewer to investigations using additional telemetry. Alignment training, access restrictions and detection of residual violations play different roles. This architecture helps explain the economics of oversight: not every event can receive the most expensive investigation. Claimed coverage, however, is not independent proof that every threat is detected. [S07](https://alignment.openai.com/how-we-detect-frontier-model-misalignment/)

Examining the infrastructure itself may matter more than the quality of an individual AI judge. In a pilot published on 22 October 2026, METR and Redwood examined OpenAI’s historical monitoring system as it stood on or before 21 July, with extensive access. They tested coverage and robustness. The authors distinguish problems found in the old system from later OpenAI claims of fixes that they did not themselves verify. ORI’s lesson is concrete: reconcile agent logs with independent telemetry, and do not present historical findings as current vulnerabilities without reassessment. [S10](https://metr.org/blog/2026-10-22-red-teaming-openai-agent-monitoring/)

Apollo Research extends the evaluation horizon further. Its position dated 1 October 2026 calls for access not only to a finished model but also to training, intermediate versions and internal use. Disappearance of visible bad behaviour may reflect improvement or more effective concealment. This motivates studying incentives and evaluation awareness rather than inferring intentions from one striking demonstration. The authors also emphasise that even deeper evaluations do not replace controls. [S11](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/blog/towards-embedded-evaluations-for-scheming-propensities)

## 4. What “proved” actually means

Formal methods start elsewhere: rather than asking whether a model is good in general, specify a property precisely and check it. Marabou, associated with Stanford, Hebrew University and collaborators, uses SMT-based methods to analyse neural networks. Marabou 2.0 appeared at CAV 2024, with code, examples and supported formats available. Within the specified model, it can prove a property or find a counterexample. But an arbitrary LLM answer does not become true because some other property was proved; supported operations, complexity and unfinished computations also matter. [S22](https://github.com/NeuralNetworkVerification/Marabou) [S23](https://theory.stanford.edu/~barrett/pubs/WIZ%2B24-abstract.html)

In cyber-physical systems, Sanjit Seshia and colleagues at UC Berkeley combine learning with explicit environment models and deductive methods. Scenic and VerifAI help describe scenarios and search for dangerous cases. VerifAI’s documentation covers simulation-guided falsification, systematic fuzzing, counterexample search and parameter synthesis. This is a powerful way to discover a failure, but an unsuccessful counterexample search does not establish that none exists: the scenario model bounds the conclusion. [S19](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~sseshia/) [S20](https://verifai.readthedocs.io/en/stable/) [S21](https://github.com/BerkeleyLearnVerify/VerifAI)

KeYmaera X and work by André Platzer, Stefan Mitsch and collaborators show how proposed control can be separated from a safeguard. A small trusted kernel checks proofs about control programs and continuous dynamics. The FM 2021 paper on a safety net for a neural-network navigation controller is particularly relevant. The connection to AI agents is clear: a model proposes, while a separate mechanism constrains action. The guarantee still depends on dynamics, initial state, measurements and execution; the contemporary research ecosystem should not be attributed to a single university. [S24](https://keymaerax.org/) [S25](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~smitsch/publications.html) [S26](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~smitsch/pdf/waypoint.pdf)

There is a narrower but important question: was an answer actually computed by the model we were promised? FlashProve from Anthropic, EPFL and collaborators, presented on 2 November 2026, concerns cryptographic verification of that computation. Code, Lean formalizations and a prototype were released; the authors describe the result as intermediate progress. This provides a provenance and computational-integrity layer under relevant assumptions, not proof that the answer is true, harmless or free of hidden goals. [S09](https://www.anthropic.com/research/provable-inference)

Harmonic’s commercial Aristotle illustrates how automated verification is reaching software and hardware developers. The announcement dated 27 October 2026 concerns C and Verilog. Specification quality and assumptions still need examination, and vendor success stories are not an independent audit. One practical detail is easy to miss: the current FAQ says Lean-only projects are not presently verified and receive an unsupported-language notice; support is only expected to return later. [S27](https://www.harmonic.fun/news/aristotle-software-hardware-verification/) [S28](https://aristotle.harmonic.fun/docs/faq)

## 5. Who is building the research foundations?

Individual tools need a broader programme: which guarantees matter, and how can they be combined? The UK’s ARIA announced a £59 million budget for Safeguarded AI. Its plan changed in May 2026, centring an open mathematical-assurance toolkit and cybersecurity through Tooling and Applications. This is funded research, not a completed safe model; the earlier general deployment API should not be described as an unchanged plan. The project directory points towards further study of Oxford / Alessandro Abate / SAINT, York / Radu Calinescu / ULTIMATE, Topos Research UK / David Jaz Myers, and Birmingham / Mirco Giacobbe. Funding establishes work, not a completed proof. [S15](https://aria.org.uk/opportunity-spaces/trust-everything-everywhere/safeguarded-ai) [S16](https://aria.org.uk/opportunity-spaces/trust-everything-everywhere/safeguarded-ai/funded-projects)

The Beneficial AI Foundation’s QAISI brings together researchers from Berkeley, MIT, Montréal, Stanford and CMU around quantitative guarantees. Named participants include Clark Barrett, Yoshua Bengio, Steve Omohundro, Bryan Parno, Stuart Russell, Dawn Song and Max Tegmark. For ORI, this network is useful primarily as a map of shared questions and researchers whose work deserves reading, not as a certified product. [S17](https://www.beneficialaifoundation.org/qaisi) [S18](https://www.beneficialaifoundation.org/research)

RESI, the Institute for Responsible Superintelligence, asks a particularly relevant question: what can a weaker observer verify about a stronger system? Its work concerns safety definitions, explicit assumptions, control, delegation, component composition and limits on possible guarantees. The institute explicitly notes that conditional guarantees do not establish safety of an entire AI system. Moving from a local property to a complete system is often the hardest step. [S29](https://resi.org/) [S30](https://resi.org/research/)

Not every interesting idea is equally open to scrutiny. Conjecture’s historical Cognitive Emulation programme proposed bounded, understandable cognitive components and complexity control; its 2024 roadmap remains a useful architectural reference. But in a retrospective dated 26 March 2026, cofounder Connor Leahy described a shift to products in 2025 and the end of this chapter of the company, so presenting CoEm as a currently proven laboratory system would be misleading. Safe Superintelligence Inc. officially aims to advance capabilities and safety together, but the page read provides insufficient technical detail for comparison with Scientist AI. That warrants watching for publications, not a technical ranking; limited public detail does not establish an absence of private progress. [S12](https://www.conjecture.dev/research/conjecture-a-roadmap-for-cognitive-software-and-a-humanist-future-of-ai) [S13](https://www.ettf.land/p/conjecture-a-retrospective) [S14](https://ssi.inc/)

## 6. Beyond the familiar laboratory map

The international picture becomes more interesting when we look for different ways to test a claim rather than additional logos. At the Chinese Academy of Sciences’ Institute of Software, TIS combines abstract interpretation, SMT, reachability and PAC assessments. DeepPAC, DeepInc, DeepCDCL and PRODeep address different tasks: DeepInc reuses verification results after small network changes, while DeepPAC uses probabilistic approximation. Tools and ISS autonomous-driving demonstrations are described. Yet a statistical guarantee has conditions, and a neural-network property is not the same as safety on a real road. [S31](https://tis.ios.ac.cn/) [S32](https://tis.ios.ac.cn/iss/pages/verification/trustworthy_ai/) [S33](https://tis.ios.ac.cn/iss/pages/open_projects/neural_network_verification.html)

In Germany, Neuro-Symbolic Verification by Xuan Xie, Kristian Kersting and Daniel Neider, published at IJCAI 2022 and listed by DFKI, incorporates neural components into formal specifications to express semantic properties. A publication and implementation based on neural-network verifiers are available. This is an important bridge between formulas and meaning, but errors in the component recognising meaning do not disappear because the rest of the system is formally correct. [S34](https://www.dfki.de/en/web/research/projects-and-publications/publication/14069) [S35](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00938)

Japan’s AIST IPRI illustrates another part of this effort: Yusuke Kawamoto’s 2025–2028 project studies mathematical verification of the adequacy of measures against machine-learning risks, while related work by Reynald Affeldt formalises physical and probabilistic systems in Rocq. The aims and projects are confirmed; releasing AI-safety and evaluation materials in May 2026 does not itself establish a general guarantee. The important development to follow is the transition from programme goals to specific, checkable results. [S36](https://www.ipri.aist.go.jp/en/projects/index.html) [S37](https://www.aist.go.jp/aist_j/press_release/pr2026/pr20260528/pr20260528.html)

Singapore’s NUS FOCS / KISP reminds us to examine proofs themselves. An audit dated 1 November 2026 considers AI-generated memory-safety proofs from Anthropic/Theorem and asks whether the proved specification matches the advertised property. No errors detected by AddressSanitizer is not equivalent to complete memory safety. This critiques a particular result, not all AI-generated proofs. A more applied layer exists alongside it: Project Moonshot from AI Verify Foundation / IMDA provides an open toolkit for benchmarking and red teaming LLMs and applications. It supports repeatable empirical checks, but passing a test suite does not rule out untested failures. [S38](https://focs-lab.comp.nus.edu.sg/) [S39](https://focs-lab.comp.nus.edu.sg/blog/memory-safety-audit/) [S40](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/tools/moonshot/) [S41](https://github.com/aiverify-foundation/moonshot)

## 7. What ORI can usefully investigate

ORI’s natural connection is scientific reliability and the safety of research tools. When an agent reads papers, writes code or proposes an experiment, the immediate questions concern evidence: where did a claim come from, how reliable is it, who authorised the action, and can the result be reproduced? Our existing reviews of [agents and evaluation](agents-and-evaluation.en.md) and [evidence and citations](evidence-and-citations.en.md) provide an appropriate starting point.

The first sensible experiment is not to build universally safe AI but to compare several safeguards on one safe synthetic task. An agent can work with fictional files, messages and calendars, without real recipients, payments or personal data. Compare no monitor, an independent monitor, deferral to a restricted agent and deterministic permission checks. Measure usefulness, missed dangerous actions, false blocks, latency and cost. This reveals which mechanism helps in which situation rather than producing a promotional safety percentage.

A second experiment separates proposals from execution. AI produces a typed operation; another process checks authority, recipient, limits and current consent; a minimal executor performs it. We can then study races between checking and action, duplicate execution, stale permissions and recovery after failure. Context history must not become indefinite authorisation. This is a concrete software-reliability and access-control question: authority to act must remain verifiable and revocable.

A third experiment asks whether the system can acknowledge uncertainty. On ambiguous and changed facts, compare calibration and abstention at different error costs. Then check whether the agent controls evaluation frequency, logs or monitor permissions, reconciling them with separate telemetry. These proposals require preregistered assumptions, data and rule versions, and negative results. They have not been carried out as part of this review; testing real external services without separate agreement is not proposed.

## 8. Where to go next

This account covers 23 organisations, programmes and research lines, not 23 independent companies. Participants and funding overlap. It includes North America, the United Kingdom, continental Europe, Israel, China, Japan and Singapore, but is not a complete global catalogue. Omitting a country or group does not imply that it lacks research. Selection reflects accessible primary technical descriptions and relevance to AI verification or control.

The next level of reading is to open the primary material for an approach and ask exactly what its authors undertake to establish. Each claim needs a date, protected object, assumed adversary, assumptions, proof or experiment, code availability and independent replication status. A theorem, probabilistic certificate, audit, passed test and promise of a future product are different outcomes. The map’s most useful shared lesson is simple: trust in AI begins not with the convincing word “safe” but with the ability to understand and test the limits of a specific promise.
