## Subject

Locke asks what remains the same through time. A mass of matter, a living organism and a person have different identity conditions. An organism sustains one life while its particles change; personal identity follows consciousness as it appropriates past actions. The same body, the same soul and the same accountable subject therefore do not automatically supply an identical criterion.

## Argument

Moving from an oak and an animal to a person makes identity depend on what kind of thing is being compared. The prince and cobbler example then separates familiar indicators: consciousness of the prince's past is imagined in another body. The discussion of person as a forensic term connects identity to owning actions, reward and punishment. This concerns grounds of responsibility, rather than the machinery of a nervous system.

## ORI critical interpretation

The programme should distinguish information about an event from experiencing it as one's own. A system might reproduce a biographical account without resolving who experienced its contents. Locke himself considers misleading representations of the past. His position cannot simply become a formula equating uploaded memories with a preserved person. Nor can the chapter's theological assumptions silently become modern empirical evidence.

## Open questions

A useful assessment asks how the criterion handles forgetting, recovered memories and two equally convincing continuers. The last is an ORI question, not an answer attributed to Locke. This chapter supplies a vocabulary for formulating the problem; it neither establishes consciousness transfer nor determines contemporary rules for digital models of people.
