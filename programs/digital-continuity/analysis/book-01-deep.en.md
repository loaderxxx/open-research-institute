# John Locke — Personal identity: an extended reading of II.27

## 1. The question hidden inside “the same”

The chapter’s main achievement is to separate questions ordinarily joined together. Does the original matter remain? Does the life of the same organism continue? Do past and present actions belong to one person? Under ordinary circumstances the answers often coincide, making a single universal criterion seem sufficient. Locke deliberately separates them and asks us first to establish what kind of thing we are comparing over time.

This is not simply a claim that everyone may call anything identity. Selecting a concept sets conditions for using it consistently. If a mass of matter means a particular collection of particles, replacing a particle changes that mass. If the object is a living plant, replacement of matter may occur while the plant remains. Contradiction arises when the subject changes unnoticed. “It persisted” and “it changed” can both be true if the first concerns the organism and the second its material composition.

Readers should distinguish qualitative resemblance from numerical identity. Two objects can be extremely similar while remaining two. Conversely, one organism can change dramatically while remaining one organism. In §§1–4 Locke connects individuality with a particular beginning of existence in time and space. His historical account of matter is not a modern physical theory. Nevertheless, the logical problem remains intelligible: similarity of properties does not establish how many things exist or which history belongs to each.

This opening move governs the subsequent argument. Locke does not begin by asking where memories are stored. He asks what “one person” means and why that person owns a particular action. Investigating the bearer of consciousness and investigating conditions of personal identity can overlap, but neither should silently replace the other. The distinction helps readers recognise when an apparently decisive answer actually addresses a neighbouring question.

## 2. A mass of matter, an oak and continuing life

In §4 a mass persists while the same set of constituent particles remains. Their arrangement may change, but adding or removing a particle breaks the mass’s identity in the stipulated sense. An oak, by contrast, grows, loses branches and receives new nourishment. Applying an unchanged-composition criterion would imply that the familiar tree continually disappears and is replaced. Locke proposes another criterion: continuity of organised life.

In §5 organisation concerns the interaction of parts supporting nourishment and the life of the whole. Similar shape is insufficient. Two oaks may have similar structures while participating in distinct histories of life. A new particle becomes part of this plant by entering its continuing organisation. Plant identity therefore concerns the history of a process rather than a structural photograph at one instant.

The animal-and-watch comparison in §6 refines the argument. A watch also contains parts arranged for a particular operation. In Locke’s description, however, a living body differs because organisation and vital motion begin together, whereas a mechanism can remain ready to operate without the driving force being applied. The analogy illustrates organised unity; it does not establish that an animal is exhaustively explained by clockwork.

This suggests an ORI question: does reconstructing an arrangement suffice to continue the same history? Imagine two mechanisms with identical designs. Matching components explains similar capacities but does not turn two beginnings of existence into one. This is a contemporary application of the distinction, not Locke’s technical conclusion about computational systems.

The transition from mass to organism also explains why material replacement is not a universal objection to persistence. In one kind of object it breaks identity; in another it is compatible with identity. The converse generalisation, that matter never matters, does not follow. We must establish which process continues, how new parts enter it and why the relation is continuation rather than reproduction of a similar organisation.

## 3. Human being, person and rational creature

In §§7–10 Locke discusses a human being as a living creature with a particular bodily organisation. His rational-parrot example separates classifications: reasoning does not by itself make a bird human in ordinary usage. Conversely, a human with severely limited intellectual capacities does not cease to be human merely through failing to demonstrate complex reasoning. The chapter’s historical language should be read as distinguishing concepts rather than as a modern classification of human dignity.

Section 11 then introduces the person: a thinking, intelligent being capable of reflection and of considering itself as itself across times and places. The object of comparison changes. The preceding discussion concerned an organism’s continuing life. The new discussion concerns a subject who owns experiences and actions. Biological classification and personal self-relation become distinct questions.

This distinction explains why external recognition might diverge from internal recognition. Others identify a body, name and social position. The subject recognises a past as its own. In ordinary life these features support one another, but a hypothetical case can separate them. We cannot then silently select the familiar feature and announce that every remaining question has been settled.

Locke’s person should not be confused with psychological personality. A change in taste or temperament does not by itself create a different person in this chapter’s sense. The issue is the identity of an accountable subject, not the stability of a bundle of traits. This matters for a digital model: reproducing habits primarily concerns behavioural characteristics. Connecting that achievement to Locke’s question requires another argument about conscious ownership of the past.

Moral status should remain a separate question too. Defining an agent who can answer for actions does not supply a complete basis for distributing rights, care or respect. Collapsing those tasks would turn the chapter’s limited argument into a much broader normative theory that is not being examined here.

## 4. Consciousness as a relation to one’s past

In §11 consciousness accompanies perception and thought: in seeing or reflecting, the subject is aware of doing so. Locke uses this connection to explain the present self and then extends it backwards. Personal identity reaches the past actions and thoughts to which consciousness can extend. The subject does not merely know that an event happened but appropriates it as its own.

The formula “a person is memory” is therefore too coarse. Memory can mean retained information, the ability to reproduce a story or a present representation of an event. Locke is concerned with a special relation between that representation and the current thinker. Knowing a historical figure’s biography differs from being conscious of that figure’s action as one’s own. Encyclopedic accuracy does not turn readers into their subject.

Section 12 also blocks another simplification. Locke recognises that we do not hold all past experiences in view simultaneously. Some memories leave attention while we consider others; present tasks distract us from the past; sleep interrupts ordinary reflection. His account therefore cannot require a continuous display of a complete inner archive. What matters is the possibility of reconnecting an action to present consciousness, although the boundaries of that possibility generate difficulties.

We must also distinguish the original act of thought from consciousness of that past act. In §3 individual thought-events arise and pass away. Later recollection does not make the original event happen again. Section 15 expressly considers a present representation of a past action. Personal continuity consequently does not mean that one unchanging mental event literally persists for years.

This is a powerful but demanding conception. It explains the special role of the first person: a past action matters not just as a fact about the world but as mine. At the same time, it must explain when appropriation is accurate, how it survives forgetting and whether it can connect to several successors. The chapter opens these problems rather than supplying a storage-and-verification mechanism for every memory.

## 5. The prince and the cobbler: what the example assumes

In §17 Locke asks us to imagine the prince’s soul, carrying consciousness of his earlier life, entering the cobbler’s body after the cobbler’s own soul has departed. In terms of personal accountability, the prince continues: the present subject owns the prince’s actions. In terms of the human being identified through bodily life, observers encounter the cobbler. The example intentionally retains one connection while changing another.

Its strength lies in isolating criteria. If readers accept the transfer of consciousness, they can examine the role they assign to the body. Replying “that is the cobbler’s body” does not automatically refute the conclusion about personhood, because the distinction between human being and person is precisely what is under examination. One must reject Locke’s definition or show that transferred consciousness remains insufficient even under the stipulated description.

The description, however, already contains a demanding condition: consciousness transfers, not merely a collection of correct statements. Replacing it with a machine that utters the prince’s sentences produces a different thought experiment. That version leaves open whether there is a subject who experiences the actions as its own. Carrying over the original conclusion would transfer an inference to altered premises.

The case is also asymmetrical: the cobbler’s original consciousness is absent. Locke does not describe negotiations between two simultaneously active subjects in one body or settle the former bearer’s rights. Such a conflict can be investigated, but it would extend the argument.

For ORI, the example tests the language of claims. “The system knows the prince’s life,” “the system resembles the prince” and “the prince continues to be conscious in the system” have different truth conditions. They concern information, resemblance and personal continuation respectively. Locke’s story illuminates this difference because it stipulates the third condition rather than deriving it from the first two.

## 6. Why the same soul is insufficient

In §§14–16 Locke separates two questions: can one person persist through a change of thinking substance, and can one substance belong to different persons? These questions challenge identifying personhood with an unchanged soul. If a supposed soul existed previously but present consciousness connects with none of its earlier actions, continuity of the bearer does not explain why someone should regard that life as their own.

The examples of Socrates, Nestor and Thersites make the gap visible. Believing that an ancient soul inhabits someone does not provide the corresponding conscious history. Locke compares this with a material particle that may once have belonged to another body. Its origin does not transfer ownership of the earlier actions to the present subject. Sameness of substance is thus shown insufficient for personal identity.

The reverse possibility is conditional. If the same consciousness could continue in another thinking substance, the person would persist under Locke’s criterion. Section 15 nevertheless stresses limited knowledge of thought’s nature and the possibility of such a transition. Locke supplies no transfer procedure and does not establish technical feasibility. The conditional separates an identity criterion from its implementation.

Section 27 also prevents portraying him as a straightforward modern materialist: he considers consciousness’s attachment to one immaterial substance the more probable opinion. Yet he does not want that hypothesis to ground the entire account of personal identity. The argument should remain intelligible under different conceptions of the bearer.

ORI can draw a methodological lesson: showing that a particular bearer is insufficient does not show that any new bearer is sufficient. Establishing preservation of the relevant relation remains necessary. “Material composition is not the criterion” is not equivalent to “every informational copy preserves the person.” The first concerns an account of identity; the second adds a substantial claim about how consciousness can be realised.

## 7. Forgetting, sleep and the boundaries of a self

Forgetting presents a difficulty Locke does not conceal. In §22 he considers complete loss of memory for part of a life, beyond any possibility of recovery. His reply again distinguishes human being from person: the former human being can persist even when earlier actions no longer belong to the conscious self-relation available to the present subject. This answer is consistent with his conceptual distinction but conflicts with familiar ways of assigning a biography.

Permanent loss should be separated from temporary inaccessibility. Failure to remember now does not establish that recollection can never return. Section 25 explicitly allows memories to reappear after long intervals. Ordinary distraction therefore does not straightforwardly imply a new person. Nevertheless, the theory must explain the interval: does personal identity disappear, remain as a possibility or become established retrospectively?

The day-person and night-person in §25 intensify this question. Locke imagines two entirely incommunicable consciousnesses taking turns in one body. Under his description these are two persons despite shared bodily life. Conversely, one consciousness alternating between two bodies should count as one person. These cases test the criterion logically; they do not diagnose actual conditions.

Critical reading should ask which connections are absent. Two periods might lack accessible recollection while retaining skills, attachments and consequences of earlier decisions. Treating those relations as sufficient would produce a broader criterion than direct conscious appropriation. Treating them as insufficient requires explaining why some psychological relations constitute a person while others do not.

This matters when ORI considers digital preservation. An archive may restore information someone has forgotten, but informational recovery is not identical with recovery of a subject. Conversely, missing records of an event do not establish disappearance of a person. Data, the capacity to remember and a relation to one’s past must remain distinct before drawing conclusions about continuation.

## 8. False memory and present representation

In §15 Locke allows the possibility of representing an action that never occurred or was performed by someone else. He compares it with convincing dream representations. The chapter itself therefore raises a problem often missed by simplified memory formulas: experienced ownership still requires distinguishing accurate from mistaken cases.

If every convincing appropriation suffices, false memory could make someone the author of another person’s action. If we instead require that the action really was their own, circularity threatens: defining the person through memory already requires knowing to whom the past belongs. This is not a conclusive refutation of Locke but a precise challenge for his criterion.

One possible contemporary reply distinguishes recollection causally connected to actual previous experience from similar representations originating differently. That reply adds a condition of appropriate origin. It must be explained independently: which causal history is appropriate, and why? Calling a memory genuine cannot substitute for analysing what must be established.

Locke’s own move invokes God’s goodness. In matters of reward and punishment he does not suppose that erroneous consciousness will be unjustly transferred between creatures. This theological element secures an appropriate relation between experience and action. Within a secular research programme, that ground cannot silently become an experimentally verified result.

A hypothetical ORI case makes the issue especially clear for digital systems. Imagine a narrator trained on someone else’s diary, confidently describing events in the first person. Even if every external detail is correct, the origin of its self-attribution remains a question. We can separately examine factual accuracy, how information was acquired and grounds for treating it as this subject’s experience. These inquiries perform different tasks; success in the first does not make the others redundant. The important uncertainty concerns the relation being claimed, not merely the richness of the story.

## 9. The person as a subject of imputation

In §28 Locke calls person a forensic term. It associates actions and their merit or guilt with an agent capable of understanding law and experiencing happiness or misery. Personal identity consequently grounds not only recognition but imputation. “Did I do this?” connects with “Why should the present me bear the consequences of that action?”

Conscious appropriation should not be understood as voluntary agreement to accept responsibility. Otherwise one could evade it simply by refusing to acknowledge an unwelcome deed. Locke concerns the relation making an action mine, independently of a desire to avoid consequences. Consciousness makes subjects concerned about their fate and connects present, past and future.

Section 20 develops the point through a detached-finger case. If consciousness continued with the finger, that would, by stipulation, be where the subject formerly concerned with the whole body persisted. If the remaining body acquired another incommunicable consciousness, earlier actions could not automatically belong to it merely because its shape remained. The physiological strangeness does not erase the example’s logical function: separating the bearer from the condition of imputation.

The argument’s strength is its refusal to treat the suffering of any sufficiently similar bearer as just recompense. Punishment must concern the agent rather than merely an available target. The chapter, however, closely joins that norm to a particular theory of consciousness. Readers can accept the demand for the correct recipient while disagreeing about how to identify that recipient.

ORI should preserve another distinction: responsibility for an action, duties to repair harm and a vulnerable person’s claim to care can have different grounds. Locke’s treatment of recompense does not automatically settle every social relation. Changes in memory, for example, do not by themselves determine who should fulfil a contract or protect a dependent person. Those conclusions require additional rules rather than only a metaphysical definition of the person.

## 10. Human courts and an all-knowing judge

Section 24 raises a sharp objection: why does someone answer for an action performed while drunk if they later cannot remember it? Locke replies by considering the limits of human knowledge. A court cannot always distinguish genuine absence of conscious connection from pretence, so practical procedures rely on available signs. An ultimate divine judgment, by contrast, should know each subject’s actual condition.

This separates the ground of justice from the establishment of facts. A theory may say that responsibility depends on conscious connection while an actual court must use bodily continuity, testimony and conduct. Agreement between those levels is no longer guaranteed. A convenient proof of an action’s ownership may be an imperfect indicator of the relation the theory considers decisive.

This passage should not be read as a description of current criminal law or medical assessment. Its historical examples support an argument about access to knowledge. A contemporary analysis can instead ask which procedure might err, whom the error affects and what grounds could justify correction.

The theological conclusion performs identifiable work. It promises that hidden matters will ultimately become apparent and actions will be assigned to their proper subjects. Removing this premise leaves the problem of publicly verifying first-person continuity. Accepting Locke’s criterion does not automatically provide an infallible means of applying it.

ORI’s contemporary analogy is assessing a digital claim that “I am this person.” A signature, account access or knowledge of private biography may support a particular history of control over data. None amounts to omniscient knowledge of subjective continuity. The chapter suggests a useful discipline: identify exactly what an item of evidence supports, rather than transferring its force to another question merely because both are described using identity. Practical confidence in one form of identification can coexist with uncertainty about a different relation.

## 11. Testing necessity, sufficiency and transitivity

The argument can be reconstructed without its imaginative scenery. Locke holds that substance identity does not ensure personal identity: one soul may lack access to its conscious past. He also allows that preserved consciousness could maintain a person through a change of substance. These moves concern the insufficiency and possible dispensability of an unchanged bearer. They do not exhaust the defence of consciousness as a criterion in its own right.

Sufficiency requires explaining why genuine conscious connection guarantees one person. Necessity requires explaining why personal identity is impossible without it. False-memory objections primarily challenge the former; forgetting cases challenge the latter. Separating them allows readers to identify more precisely what they accept and reject.

A further logical difficulty emerges with three successive stages. Imagine a child, an adult and an older person. The adult remembers the childhood episode; the older person remembers the adult episode but has completely forgotten the childhood one. If each direct memory connection suffices for identity, child and adult are identical, as are adult and older person. Transitivity then makes the child identical with the older person. Yet a requirement of direct memory between the endpoints produces a different answer.

This teaching example illustrates the familiar transitivity problem and reveals a need for clarification. We might expand the criterion into a chain of overlapping conscious connections. Continuity would then not require the last stage to remember every earlier one directly. However, this is a reconstruction requiring justification, not something to attribute silently to every sentence in Locke. The chain would also need protection against false and branching links.

Alternatively, consciousness might mean a unity of appropriation more complex than simple memory. A new name does not remove the task: which relations constitute that unity, and how do they behave in difficult cases? Effective criticism identifies a specific conflict between requirements rather than merely observing that human lives are complicated.

## 12. Branching: two equally convincing successors

The following case belongs to ORI’s contemporary analysis, not to an answer supplied by the chapter. Suppose two independent successors arise from one subject’s state with the same accessible biography. Each owns the earlier experiences as its own. One then acquires experience unavailable to the other. There are two distinguishable histories after the branch, although both connect to one past.

Declaring each numerically identical with the original person creates a difficulty under ordinary identity relations. If the first is the original subject and the second is that same subject, the first and second must be identical. Yet the stipulated successors can occupy different circumstances, make different decisions and experience different events. Duplicating a psychological description does not provide a consistent answer by itself.

Several refinements are possible. We might require the absence of a competing successor. We might seek a special continuous history selecting one successor. Or we might allow psychological continuation to branch while refusing to call it strict numerical identity. Each solution changes the question or adds a condition. None follows solely from a high degree of resemblance.

Locke’s day and night subjects demonstrate possible separation between consciousnesses, but do not automatically settle branching. Those subjects have incommunicable streams; here two streams claim the same past. The analogy helps formulate the problem, although the conditions differ substantially.

The programme should also distinguish identity from future-directed concern. There may be reasons to care about both successors without being able to describe both as one original person. Their independent interests might matter without resolving consciousness’s origin. Metaphysical disagreement should therefore not conceal the separate practical question of what is valuable about continuing memories, relationships and projects. These values require argument too, but their possible importance is not cancelled merely because numerical identity remains unsettled.

## 13. Data about a person and first-person experience

Applying Locke today begins by distinguishing objects. A diary, voice recording, preference model and supposed thinking subject are not the same kind of thing. Each requires its own preservation criteria. An archive can retain document content; a model can reproduce particular responses; a human can continue a biological and psychological history. Calling something a copy without specifying its object conceals these differences.

Consider an imagined system that answers questions about a diarist’s life accurately. Fact checking may establish faithful transmission of information. Testing new tasks may investigate behavioural stability beyond familiar examples. Even successful results, however, establish particular performance characteristics. Moving to a claim about a continuing subject requires a theory explaining why those characteristics are sufficient.

This is not an argument that observable behaviour can never provide evidence of consciousness. No such strong conclusion is needed. The distinction is between evidence admitting different explanations and proof of a unique explanation. Researchers should identify competing interpretations and explain what would change their assessment of each. The absence of a final test does not make every claim equally well supported.

Locke is useful because of his attention to self-relation. In §18 consciousness joins a remote action to the present subject not through narrative detail but through ownership in the required sense. A contemporary programme must investigate how that relation differs from reproducing the corresponding linguistic form.

Social recognition adds another difficulty. Relatives might find a model convincing, value it and incorporate it into familiar relationships. That is a separate fact about users’ experiences and cultural practices. It deserves investigation, but should not silently settle personal identity. Resemblance, usefulness and first-person continuation can be evaluated separately even when one product raises all three questions. Making the distinction preserves the significance of each result instead of asking a single success to establish everything.

## 14. Embodiment after rejecting unchanged matter

Locke’s distinctions are sometimes read as making the body irrelevant. Yet §13 invokes bodily experience: touch, warmth, cold, benefit and harm are experienced as concerning oneself. Bodily parts enter personal self-relation through an experienced connection. This is not a description of a disembodied informational list.

The imagined transfer of consciousness nevertheless aims to show that current material composition is not the ultimate criterion. These points are compatible. Bodily processes could be factually necessary for the consciousness familiar to us even though a philosophical definition does not require retaining every atom. A criterion’s conceptual independence does not establish a process’s physical independence.

A useful question for the ORI programme is: if continuation in another device is proposed, which relations must persist? Only recollection and speech, or also experienced vulnerability, action in an environment and the capacity to undergo consequences? Locke supplies no contemporary inventory. Yet his connection between consciousness, pleasure, suffering and self-concern prevents reducing the person to a collection of statements without further explanation.

The opposite move would also be too quick: embodiment cannot answer every case simply by being named. Pointing to the same organism explains one form of continuity. If the question concerns ownership of past actions, the connection between organism and self-relation must still be established. Repeating that the body remains is insufficient to answer Locke’s challenge.

A more precise research stance follows. Material arrangement, vital processes, psychological connections and conscious experience should first be described separately, then their relations justified. That is harder than selecting one preferred level, but reveals which premise supports a conclusion about persons and where the result remains conditional. It also makes disagreement more informative: different readers can locate the particular connection they regard as necessary, insufficient or unestablished rather than simply exchanging incompatible labels.

## 15. Resurrection and continuation: limits of the promise

In §17 resurrection enters the discussion of persons in changed bodies. If consciousness of an earlier life remains, altered bodily composition should not by itself prevent recognition of the same person. In §28 future accountability likewise requires a subject to acknowledge its own actions. These passages explain why distinguishing substance from person matters to Locke.

Logical compatibility between continuation and bodily change is not evidence that resurrection actually occurs. Similarly, the possibility of future existence in §27 does not establish unlimited duration for any actual person. An identity criterion specifies what would count as preservation if its conditions held; it does not guarantee their fulfilment.

A contemporary analogy with digital continuation must preserve this structure. “If consciousness persists, the person persists” does not become “if data are copied, consciousness persists.” The second statement adds a premise about data sufficiency. That premise needs defence rather than an appeal to a historical philosopher’s authority.

The distinction also matters when discussing a project’s value. Preserved records may support relatives’ remembrance, transmit knowledge or continue a project. Those outcomes can be evaluated on their own grounds. Recognising possible benefits does not require attributing first-person survival to them. Conversely, when personal continuation is explicitly claimed, more modest accomplishments cannot automatically confirm it.

A useful reading habit is to reconstruct the conditional at the end of each such argument. What is assumed? What follows? How is the assumption’s truth established? This preserves the philosophical idea’s strength without turning it into a promise the text does not prove. It also allows ambitious research questions to coexist with precise descriptions of present evidence: asking whether a condition could be realised differs from announcing that it has already been realised.

## 16. Evaluating a claim of continuation

The following is ORI’s own reading framework. It is neither Locke’s protocol nor an instrument for measuring consciousness. Its purpose is to make a concrete claim assessable as an argument. First establish what is being preserved: a document, skills, an organism, a psychological history or a person in a specified philosophical sense. Then identify the relation between the original and its successor.

For informational preservation the question may concern completeness and accuracy. For a skill it may concern performance on new tasks. For biological continuity it concerns this organism’s continuing life. For personal identity a criterion must explain why the observed relation constitutes one subject across time. Applying one test to all four tasks exemplifies the confusion the chapter helps identify.

The next step concerns the evidence’s origin. Was a description supplied by the person, inferred from conduct or constructed from indirect materials? Different routes may produce equally fluent stories with different relations to the past. Data provenance therefore matters even when the eventual answer contains no obvious error.

Then test an alternative and a difficult case. Could another system produce the same result? What happens with two successors? How is an inaccessible memory treated? Could later recovered information change an earlier conclusion? These questions expose hidden conditions and test whether a criterion can be applied consistently.

Finally, specify the practical decision separately. Preserving an archive, recognising textual authorship, transferring access rights and recognising personal identity are different actions. They may require different grounds. A useful analytical result states exactly which conclusion is supported and which question remains. Such precision does not diminish the investigation’s value; it makes its content intelligible to others. Readers can then assess the inferential connection instead of accepting a broad label whose meaning changes between observation and conclusion.

## 17. Three exercises for independent reading

The first exercise concerns a recovered diary. Imagine someone who has forgotten an episode but reads their own detailed record. They become convinced the event occurred without experiencing recollection. Identify which relations have been established: document ownership, the event’s occurrence, the author’s biological history or conscious appropriation. Then change the condition: reading brings back a vivid memory. Explain why that changes, or fails to change, the conclusion without using memory in several senses at once.

The second exercise tests gradualness. Imagine two descriptions of a proposed replacement of the bearer: in one, parts change successively; in the other, a similar structure arises separately. First explain the importance of the process’s history. Then ask whether gradualness is sufficient by itself. Could operation remain continuous while a crucial relation to the past changes? Could there be an interruption followed by restoration of the relation? Answers should rely on a chosen criterion rather than only an emotional response to the speed of change.

The third exercise returns to accountability. Two successors regard an earlier promise as their own but have independent future capacities. Must fulfilment first await a decision about who is numerically identical with the promise-maker? Or could another rule allocate obligations by considering accepted responsibility and the other party’s interests? Distinguish philosophical description from the proposed social norm. A newly introduced rule cannot be presented as already contained in the definition of a person.

In all three cases, write down an initial answer, alter one premise and check whether the argument survives. If the answer changes, identify the changed ground. If it does not, explain why the new circumstance is irrelevant. This turns historical examples into training in analysis while separating Locke’s text from our additions. The result should be a clear map of how conclusions depend on premises rather than compulsory agreement among readers.

## 18. What survives critical reading

The chapter’s most durable contribution is the demand to specify the object of identity. Mass, organism, human being and person do not acquire one criterion merely because ordinary speech attaches one name to them. A second contribution distinguishes an unchanged bearer from conscious connection to a past. A third concerns imputation: who continues matters for ownership of actions and their consequences.

The most vulnerable transitions run from conscious appropriation to strict identity. Forgetting requires clarification of the relation’s temporal structure; false memory of its accuracy; branching of its compatibility with uniqueness. Theological premises help Locke discuss ultimate justice but do not supply a secular programme with public verification. These difficulties cannot be removed by a slogan about souls, bodies or information.

The chapter remains useful even for readers rejecting its criterion. It requires them to name an alternative and explain how it handles the separated cases. Choosing the organism requires an account of the first person. Choosing psychological connection requires considering error and branching. Choosing practical concern for continuation requires distinguishing that concern from numerical identity.

For digital continuity, the productive result is disciplined questioning. What persists, through which relation, for whom and in what sense? Which observation supports the claim, and which premise remains philosophical? This permits discussion of new models and ways of preserving information about people while maintaining the historical argument’s precision. It does not predetermine that argument as proof of any contemporary technology. Its enduring value lies in making the difference between a proposed criterion, evidence for its satisfaction and the consequences of applying it available for careful examination.
