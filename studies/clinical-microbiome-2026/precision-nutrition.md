# Personalised nutrition: what does the stool result add?

[← Review contents](README.md)


A programme that collects a stool sample, records meals, measures glucose and offers individual feedback can be evaluated in a trial. Even a successful trial of that programme leaves a second question: would the same service work as well without the stool result? The distinction matters to patients asked to pay for testing.

## Two randomised comparisons

**Prediabetes.** Ben-Yacov and colleagues randomised 225 adults to personalised postprandial glucose-targeted advice or a Mediterranean diet. Two hundred completed six months. The personalised algorithm incorporated clinical and microbiome features. Daily time above 140 mg/dL and HbA1c improved more with the personalised programme. The comparator was an active dietary intervention, which makes this a meaningful test of a programme. Neither arm received the identical algorithm with the microbial inputs removed. A follow-on analysis of the same participants linked dietary changes, microbial composition and metabolic changes. These are two analyses of one trial population, so they should count once when judging replication. [Randomised trial, Diabetes Care](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc21-0162) · [Linked microbiome analysis, Gut](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37137684/)

**Broader cardiometabolic outcomes.** An 18-week trial compared an app-based personalised programme with general dietary advice. Among 347 participants in the intention-to-treat analysis, triglycerides fell by an additional 0.13 mmol/L in the personalised arm. The other primary endpoint, LDL cholesterol, showed no significant between-group difference. The intervention bundled health history, food scoring, postprandial measurements, microbial data and behavioural feedback. The funder, ZOE, participated in study design, analysis and writing. Independent replication against equally intensive advice would address two sources of uncertainty at once. [Trial results, methods and disclosures](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11271409/)

## Implications for a UAE service

A useful evaluation would give participants the same locally relevant food database, clinician time and app support, then compare decisions with and without microbial inputs. It should record how often advice actually changes, which meals are affected, whether patients can follow it, the cost of the extra test, and the change in a pre-specified metabolic outcome. A locally incomplete food database could degrade either programme and make the microbial assay appear more or less useful than it is.

Dietary benefit, assay value and longevity remain separate claims. A short-term change in glucose or triglycerides cannot establish fewer cardiovascular events or longer life. The strongest current question for an interested clinic is whether testing changes a decision enough to justify its cost for a defined group of patients.
