# Can microbial markers improve selection for endoscopy?

[← Review contents](README.md)


The most informative comparison is an ordinary screening participant who already has a positive faecal immunochemical test (FIT). A model that separates advanced cancer from healthy volunteers answers a different question. The clinician needs to know whether the model finds important lesions that the existing pathway would miss, and what happens to everyone classified as low risk.

## Colorectal neoplasia

| Study and population | What investigators found | What the result establishes |
|---|---|---|
| [Birkeland et al., Nature Communications, 2026, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13507204/#Tab2). 1,034 FIT-positive screening participants in Norway. | In the held-out subset of 206, the AUROC for neoplastic findings was 0.61 (95% CI 0.53–0.69) for FIT plus age, sex and lifestyle score. Adding microbial information yielded 0.64 (0.56–0.72). For cancer specifically, quantitative FIT alone performed better than the combined microbial/FIT model in the authors’ training analysis. | The potential gain depends on the lesion and comparator. The test subset came from the same study. Uncertainty around the *paired difference* cannot be inferred from the two marginal confidence intervals. No patient outcome from deferring colonoscopy was measured. |
| [Byrd et al., British Journal of Cancer, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41416-026-03495-x). Turin screening programme, 44 cancers, 269 advanced adenomas, 134 early adenomas and 478 FIT-negative controls. | Several microbial measures differed across lesion groups. Oral-associated species were more frequent in cancer, while some diversity measures were inversely associated with early adenomas. | Residual FIT material can support population research. FIT-negative controls have a different selection and verification pathway from colonoscopy-confirmed cases. These associations do not supply a safe clinical threshold. |
| [Severino et al., Microorganisms, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13413819/). Prospective Italian multicentre feasibility study. | Of 315 people screened, 236 were eligible, 212 agreed and 200 completed the procedures. Recent users of several commonly prescribed or consumed products were excluded. | Feasibility was high among eligible volunteers. The study reported no microbiome-derived diagnostic performance at this stage. General practice would include people the study excluded. |

**What changes the interpretation?** Positive FIT selects for bleeding. In the Norwegian population, some signals formerly associated with cancer appeared in participants without neoplastic findings. A test that recognises bleeding, medication exposure, or a late tumour environment can perform well in a discovery study and contribute little in a referral population. The distinction between invasive cancer, advanced adenoma, serrated lesions and any neoplastic finding matters just as much as the model. [Norwegian study, results and discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13507204/)

**A decision worth evaluating.** In consecutive FIT-positive referrals, could a pre-specified score improve the order of colonoscopy appointments under a fixed capacity, while everyone still receives standard follow-up? A credible report would show cancers and advanced lesions by risk group, calibration by site, and days to detection. A later decision to withhold a procedure would require a separate safety evaluation. The research question here concerns prioritisation only.

## Barrett’s oesophagus

A 2026 analysis used 505 oesophageal tissue samples and compared ecological patterns across normal tissue, Barrett’s oesophagus and adenocarcinoma. The investigators also considered Barrett’s cases with different outcomes over long follow-up. Their model identified differences in community assembly. A sample count is not a person count, and retrospective separation of disease states does not establish prospective prediction for an individual with Barrett’s. A separate small study included only nine people with Barrett’s and examined saliva, gastric juice and stool. These specimen types answer different biological questions. [Guccione et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41667953/) · [Reflux and Barrett’s study, 2026 issue](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1684118225002178)

The 2025 American Gastroenterological Association surveillance guideline provides the clinical comparator: high-quality endoscopy, imaging and structured tissue assessment. The open question is whether a microbial signal can improve prediction of *incident* high-grade dysplasia or adenocarcinoma beyond established clinical and histological information. That would require a time horizon, an independently followed cohort, and enough progression events to estimate missed cases. The papers reviewed here do not establish a surveillance interval based on a microbial result. [AGA guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41125322/)
