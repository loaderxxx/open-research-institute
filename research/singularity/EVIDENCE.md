# Evidence and disagreements: what exactly is accelerating?

[Track overview](README.md) · [Русский](EVIDENCE.ru.md)

The propositions below do not share an endpoint. A philosophical argument can describe a mechanism; a survey measures beliefs; an experiment can measure a particular outcome. None substitutes for the others.

### A conjecture is not a threshold test

[Vinge's 1993 conference paper](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19940022856) describes a historical break following the emergence of greater-than-human intelligence. [Chalmers's 2010 analysis](https://consc.net/papers/singularity.pdf) separates machines designing more capable successors from the distinct claim that the design cycle itself gets faster. The disputed link is not whether machines can help design machines. It is whether each round makes the next sufficiently easier to outweigh new bottlenecks. Neither argument supplies a measured, irreversible threshold.

### Three kinds of empirical evidence

- **Benchmark performance.** [Kwa et al., task-horizon study, v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499v4) estimate the human expert task duration at which an AI system succeeds half the time. In the historical suite studied, this horizon roughly doubled every seven months. The unit is human time on a task, not the time an agent operated. A 50% benchmark success rate is not unattended production reliability; the historical trend is not a forecast of its own continuation.
- **AI accelerating AI research.** In its [October 2026 assessment of Anthropic](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/), METR estimates that growth on an internal capability index was 1.9 times as fast after October 2025 as before (95% interval: 1.5–2.8). That interval concerns the change in benchmark slope, **not** the fraction caused by AI. The evaluators judge assistance important but highlight uncertainty, one lab and extensive redaction. Autonomous research contributions did not yet explain a large part of progress. A faster human-led laboratory is evidence worth taking seriously, but it is not a self-sustaining loop.
- **Realized productivity.** A [field study of 5,172 customer-support agents](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044) reported 15% more issues resolved per hour on average after AI assistance was introduced. In a [randomized early-2025 coding study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089v2), 16 experienced maintainers took 19% longer when allowed AI on 246 tasks in familiar projects. A [later update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) warns that participant selection and time measurement made its new effect estimates unreliable. These results do not cancel: the tasks, users, dates and outcome units differ. Neither is a general estimate for today's work.

### Why a date changes when the question changes

In [Grace et al.'s survey of 2,778 AI researchers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843v3), the aggregate forecast reached 50% by 2047 for unaided machines outperforming humans on every task, conditional on scientific progress continuing, versus 2116 for full automation of all occupations. These are elicited forecasts for *different definitions*, not two observed dates for a singularity. Neither can tell us when organizations actually adopt a given tool.

**How ORI would update.** Repeated independently audited gains on unchanged tasks, with human oversight, costs and experiment failures disclosed, would strengthen the acceleration case. Vanishing gains on held-out tasks or rising review time would weaken the inference that gains translate into useful work. A benchmark revision, additional compute or a newly selected workforce can mimic a change in trend. Distinguishing among those explanations matters more than fitting a steeper curve.

**Scope:** targeted reading as of 19 November 2026. We checked the original essays, author/publisher abstracts and the assessors' own reports. We did not replicate experiments, inspect proprietary data or conduct a systematic review.
