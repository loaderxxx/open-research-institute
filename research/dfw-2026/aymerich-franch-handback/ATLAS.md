## Foundational evidence

[Aymerich-Franch et al., Public acceptance of cybernetic avatars in the service sector](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2025.1719342), journal study, 2026. The Dubai survey yielded 1,001 valid responses; quota recruitment covered gender and six community clusters, not a probability sample. Respondents rated still images and hypothetical tasks. The article reports 72.5% approval for information and 43.2% for handling complaints. These descriptive contrasts are not population estimates or evidence of actual use. A single respondent's opinion about multiple scenarios is not a set of independent observations. Companion analyses of this panel should not be counted as replications. Source basis: archive-reviewed methods and results, and independently located [publisher article PDF mirror](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/2489/a4b8c2f8952c7e01dc822edea91c3490f7f4.pdf).

[Aymerich-Franch & Gómez, socially assistive robots in healthcare](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2024.100465), publisher reference, archive abstract review. Their European survey offers a different set of scenarios, including comparatively sensitive access and care decisions. It helps ask whether acceptance varies with perceived stakes. Context, recruitment and tasks are different; the findings should not be pooled with the Dubai percentages. Neither study tests actual cancellation.

## Adjacent evidence

| [Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos, cognitive forcing experiments](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692) | Randomized experiment contrasting deliberation-inducing interface designs with simple explanations and an unaided comparison condition. Authors report reduced overreliance under cognitive forcing and a subjective usability tradeoff under some designs. | Detecting and rejecting a bad AI recommendation does not establish that cancellation is reliable. | [Kocielnik, Amershi & Bennett, expectation setting for imperfect AI](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/chi19_kocielnik_et_al.pdf) | Tests expectations in an email-based meeting detection workflow; satisfaction depends on how expectations are framed and what type of errors the assistant makes. | A better initial expectation does not prove a user can stop an agent during a mode switch. | [Amershi et al., Human–AI Interaction Guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300233) | Authors propose and validate a design framework including easy dismissal, correction and graceful degradation under uncertainty. | Design guidelines guide interface hypotheses; they do not validate a specific avatar or determine its effect size. |

## Cross-analysis

**Three kinds of control must not be collapsed.** Information control is knowing the current source and stage; decision control is choosing, correcting or declining a recommendation; execution control is stopping or reassigning an action. A person can prefer the avatar, accurately know that software spoke, and still be unable to block a pending action. Conversely, an effective stop control is undermined if it is hard to find or fails to confirm the stopped state.

**Adoption is not the result to optimize.** An avatar that prompts less often may be preferred on routine tasks. If the comparison neglects an irreversible or time-sensitive boundary, preference cannot tell us whether the delegation is desirable.

**Evidence strength:** moderate for task-specific acceptance and the plausibility of interface effects; none yet for the proposed Dubai concierge intervention. Search was targeted literature mapping, not a systematic review or pooled effect estimate.
